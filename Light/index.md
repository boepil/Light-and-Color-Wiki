### What this section is about

Before the eye interprets anything, and before a pigment reflects anything, there is **light itself** — energy traveling through space, sometimes glowing from a source, sometimes bouncing off a surface. **This section is about the physics of that light**: what wavelengths are, why a prism splits white into a rainbow, why a screen and a painted wall behave differently, and why daylight at noon looks nothing like daylight at sunset.

The pages below go deep on each topic. This page only explains what each one is *for* and where to start.

### The pages, one by one

#### [[Wave Nature]] — what light actually is

Light is **electromagnetic radiation** — energy carried by oscillating electric and magnetic fields. It can be described as a wave (with wavelength and frequency) or as individual packets of energy called **photons**. Shorter wavelengths carry more energy per photon; longer wavelengths carry less. This page is the foundation: if you want to understand what "wavelength" means before anything else in the wiki makes sense, start here.

#### [[The Visible Spectrum]] — the narrow band your eye can see

Only a tiny slice of all electromagnetic radiation is visible — roughly from violet through red. **[[Newton's Influence on Painters|Newton]]** showed that white sunlight is not a single color but a **mixture of many wavelengths**, separated by a prism into the familiar rainbow. Some colors you see — like **magenta** — do not correspond to a single wavelength at all; they exist only as mixtures. This page answers: "What is the rainbow, physically, and where are its edges?"

#### [[Reflection vs. Emission]] — glowing vs. bouncing

Light can **come from** a source (the sun, a flame, a screen pixel) or **bounce off** a surface (a wall, a leaf, wet paint). Those are fundamentally different situations — and they explain why a monitor can glow with colors no paint can match, why highlights on a glossy object behave differently from matte paper, and why "black" on a screen means no light while "black" paint is just very dark gray. Essential if you wonder why screens and pigments feel like different worlds.

#### [[Illuminants & Correlated Color Temperature]] — naming and comparing types of light

Not all white light is the same. Candlelight is warm and orange; overcast sky is cool and blue. Color scientists describe light sources with standard **illuminants** (reference descriptions like "average daylight") and **color temperature** — a single number that captures whether a light feels warm or cool. This page explains why the same red shirt looks different under a tungsten bulb, a window, and a computer monitor — and why color measurement needs to specify which light it was measured under.

#### [[Natural Daylight Variation & Hyperspectral Scene Data]] — how daylight changes through the day, and how we record it

Daylight is not one fixed thing. At sunrise the atmosphere strips away blue and leaves red and gold; at noon the sun is whiter and harsher; open sky is bluer than direct sun because of **Rayleigh scattering** — air molecules scatter short blue wavelengths more than long red ones. This page also covers how complete spectral measurements ("hyperspectral" data) capture a surface's full light fingerprint — the raw material behind color matching and gamut analysis in **Colors**.

#### [[Spectral Locus & Excitation Purity]] — the outer boundary of physical color

In color measurement, the **spectral locus** is the horseshoe-shaped edge of all pure rainbow wavelengths — the most saturated colors light alone can produce. The straight **line of purples** closes the shape with magentas that are mixtures, not single wavelengths. **Excitation purity** (think of it as "how much pure spectral color is in the mix versus white") describes how vivid a light color is within that boundary. This is the most technical page in the section — visit it when you are ready to connect physics to the diagrams in **Colors**.

### How the pieces fit together

These pages follow light from nature to measurement: **Wave Nature** (what it is) → **Visible Spectrum** (what we see) → **Reflection vs. Emission** (sources vs. surfaces) → **Illuminants** (comparing types of light) → **Natural Daylight** (how real daylight shifts, and how we record spectra) → **Spectral Locus** (the physical outer edge of color space).

If you are visiting for the first time: start with **[[Wave Nature]]** for the physical foundation, **[[The Visible Spectrum]]** if the rainbow is your entry point, or **[[Reflection vs. Emission]]** if you mainly want to understand why screens and paint behave so differently.
