**Scope:** Covers the biological anatomy of the eye, specifically the cornea, lens, and retina.


The visual process begins when light enters the **cornea**, a transparent bulge at the front of the eye that provides primary focusing power and protection. Light then passes through the **aqueous humor** and the **pupil**, an opening in the circular **iris** that functions like a camera's aperture by constricting or dilating to regulate light levels. Behind the iris is the **lens**, which adjusts its shape—a process called **accommodation**—via the ciliary muscles to focus objects at various distances.

After traveling through the transparent **vitreous humor**, the light strikes the **retina**, the neural membrane lining the back of the eyeball. The retina contains approximately 126 million **[[Wavelength Perception|photoreceptors]]** that convert photons into electrical nerve impulses. The **fovea centralis** is a tiny pit at the center of the retina containing a high density of cones but **[[Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution|no rods]]**, making it the region of highest color acuity and sharpest vision.

### The Retina's Layers: the "inverted" design

The retina is built back-to-front relative to the light it detects — an arrangement the corpus describes as the photoreceptors "pointing backward" or being at the "wrong end". Light entering through the vitreous first encounters the **ganglion cells** (the innermost layer, whose axons gather to form the optic nerve), then the **bipolar cells**, and only at the back of the retina does it reach the **photoreceptors**. The light-sensitive **outer segments** of the rods and cones sit at the very back, apposed to the **retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)** — a single opaque layer of pigmented cells — with the **choroid**, a strongly absorbing vascular layer, behind them supplying blood to the photoreceptors.

- **Why the inverted design?** The retina develops embryonically as a protrusion of brain tissue, and the photoreceptors must lie against the RPE because it supplies the enzymes and proteins needed to **regenerate photopigments bleached by light**. The RPE is opaque, so the only way to keep the receptors in contact with it is to put the neural layers in front of them — forcing light to cross the ganglion and bipolar layers first.
- **The fovea clears the path:** at the **fovea centralis** the overlying neural layers thin into a tiny pit, and retinal blood vessels are displaced around it, leaving the foveal cones an unobstructed, low-scattering route to the incoming light — a key part of why central acuity is the sharpest in the eye.
- **Reading the diagram:** the schematic shows the cross-section as **ganglion cells → bipolar cells → photoreceptors → pigment epithelium → Bruch's membrane → choroid capillaries** — light entering from the top, neural signals leaving the bottom.

> [!NOTE] **Terminology flag:** the phrase "inverted retina" and the "Bruch's membrane" label are standard anatomical terms but are *not verbatim* in the notebook corpus — the sources describe the design as "pointing backward" / photoreceptors at the "wrong end", and do not name Bruch's membrane. The layer-by-layer arrangement itself (ganglion → bipolar → photoreceptor → pigment epithelium → choroid) is directly supported.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's approach to ocular anatomy focuses heavily on the uneven distribution and overlapping spectral sensitivities of the L, M, and S cones. He points out that our photoreceptors are not perfectly spaced to cover the visible spectrum evenly; rather, they are heavily clustered toward the longer (red/green) wavelengths.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: Biological Asymmetry vs. Symmetrical Color Spaces**
> Traditional models and uniform color spaces often imply mathematically symmetrical spectral divisions to create neat color wheels. Handprint explicitly flags that because the L, M, and S cones are heavily clumped and unevenly distributed, mathematical symmetry in color spaces is a biological falsehood. This anatomical imbalance is the biological root of our perceptual bias toward "warm" colors and our extreme sensitivity to green hues, explaining why perfectly symmetrical color spaces cannot accurately model human vision without severe distortion.
