**Scope:** How the eye translates physical wavelengths of light into the psychological experience of color — the anatomy of the visual pathway, the dual rod/cone receptor systems and their spatial distribution, the physical limits of visual acuity, wavelength sampling by three cone classes, and opponent-process recoding in the retina and brain.

### [[Anatomy|Eye/Anatomy]]
Light enters the **cornea**, the transparent bulge at the front of the eye that provides primary focusing power and physical protection, then passes through the **aqueous humor**, the **pupil** (an aperture in the circular **iris** that constricts or dilates to regulate light), and the **lens**, whose shape the ciliary muscles adjust in the process of **accommodation** to focus objects at different distances (*Contemporary Color*, *Illusions of Seeing*, *Vision Science: Photons to Phenomenology*). After traversing the transparent **vitreous humor**, light strikes the **retina**, a neural membrane lining the back of the eyeball that contains roughly **126 million photoreceptors** converting photons into electrical nerve impulses. The **fovea centralis** — a tiny pit at the retina's center packed with cones and devoid of rods — is the region of highest color acuity and sharpest vision.

### [[Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution|Eye/Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution]]
The retina's two photoreceptor classes are functionally and spatially distinct systems. **Rods** (~120 million per eye) provide achromatic, low-resolution **scotopic** (night) vision; **cones** (5–8 million per eye) mediate color vision and high detail under **photopic** (daylight) conditions. Rods regenerate the pigment rhodopsin slowly (~30 min) while cones recover faster with iodopsin (~6 min). Distribution is inverted around the fovea: cones peak at ~150,000–160,000/mm² at 0° eccentricity (the foveal center), while rods are entirely absent from the ~2° rod-free fovea and peak at ~18°–20° eccentricity. Peripheral rods converge **1,000:1** onto a single ganglion fiber — maximizing sensitivity at the cost of resolution — whereas foveal cones enjoy a near 1:1 "private line" (*Vision Science: Photons to Phenomenology*, *Illusions of Seeing*).

### [[Visual Acuity & Receptor Spacing|Eye/Visual Acuity & Receptor Spacing]]
Acuity is physically bounded by **inter-receptor spacing**: in the fovea this is ~2 µm, and resolving a detail requires an "on-off-on" pattern across at least three photoreceptors — a **sampling limit** analogous to the Nyquist argument. Normal 20/20 vision resolves about **1 minute of arc** (the Snellen E's stroke width); special resolution techniques like **vernier** achieve **3.6 arcseconds**, far below letter thresholds. Because the fovea is rod-free, direct fixation of faint objects yields a scotopic blind spot, and the ~2 µm cone packing approaches diffraction limits of the eye's optics. Continual **microsaccades** are required to keep static images from fading (*Vision Science: Photons to Phenomenology*, *Illusions of Seeing*).

### [[Wavelength Perception|Eye/Wavelength Perception]]
Color vision rests on the **Trichromatic Theory**: three distinct cone photoreceptor types sample different portions of the spectrum (*Illusions of Seeing*, *Color for Science, Art, and Technology*). While approximately **120 million rods** operate in low light for monochromatic night vision, roughly **6–7 million cones** mediate color perception in daylight:

- **S (short-wave) cones** peak at ~440–448 nm (blue-violet); the rarest class, only 5–10% of the cone population (*A Comprehensive Overview of Color Vision Mechanisms, Color Spaces, and the Munsell System*).
- **M (medium-wave) cones** peak at ~518–540 nm (green).
- **L (long-wave) cones** peak at ~560–617 nm (red-orange).

Because every cone obeys the **principle of univariance** — a single receptor's output is a scalar value that cannot distinguish a change in wavelength from a change in intensity — color is decoded from the *relative pattern of activation* across the three overlapping cone systems, not from any individual receptor (*A Comprehensive Overview…*, *The Science of Paintings*).

### [[Opponent-Process Color Coding|Eye/Opponent-Process Color Coding]]
The second stage of color vision neurally re-encodes the cone signals into antagonistic pairs: **red–green**, **blue–yellow**, and **black–white**. This opponent processing begins in the retina's **bipolar and ganglion cells** and continues to the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the brain (*A Comprehensive Overview…*, *Contemporary Color*, *Illusions of Seeing*). The **red–green channel** is computed as the difference between L and M cone excitations (**L–M**), while the **blue–yellow channel** compares S-cone input to the combined L+M signal (**S–(L+M)**). Opponent coding explains why a color is never perceived as simultaneously "reddish-green," and it accounts for **complementary afterimages**: staring at a saturated color habituates one side of a pair, shifting the balance toward the opponent hue on a neutral surface (*Color Vision: Trichromatic and Opponent Process Theories*, *The Science of Paintings*). Modern **Zone (dual-process) theories** reconcile trichromacy and opponent process as sequential stages — trichromatic at the receptor level, opponent at the neural level (*Illustrating Color Evolution and Color Blindness*).
