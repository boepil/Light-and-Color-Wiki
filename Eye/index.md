### What this section is about

Light from the world reaches your eye as physical energy — wavelengths you never see directly. **This section is about what happens inside the eye and the early brain** to turn that light into sight: sharp detail or blurry periphery, black-and-white night vision or full daylight color, and the strange effects (like afterimages) that follow. The pages below go deep on each step; this page only explains what each one is *for* and where to start.

### The pages, one by one

#### [[Anatomy|Eye/Anatomy]] — the pathway light travels

A tour of the eye as hardware: light enters through the **cornea**, passes the **pupil** (the opening the iris widens or narrows), is focused by the **lens**, and lands on the **retina** at the back — a thin layer packed with light-sensitive cells. The **fovea**, a tiny pit at the center of your gaze, is where your sharpest, most detailed vision lives. If you want to see the whole machine before anything else, start here.

#### [[Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution|Eye/Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution]] — two kinds of light detectors

The retina has two main cell types with different jobs. **Rods** are numerous and sensitive — they let you see in dim light, but only in shades of gray. **Cones** are fewer but sharper — they give you color and fine detail in bright light. They are not spread evenly: cones cluster at the center of your gaze, rods dominate the edges. That is why you see color and detail best where you look directly, and why a faint star disappears when you stare straight at it.

#### [[Visual Acuity & Receptor Spacing|Eye/Visual Acuity & Receptor Spacing]] — why some things look sharp and others blur

**How finely you can see a detail** — a letter on an eye chart, a thread, a distant face — depends on how closely the light detectors are packed and how the brain samples their signals. The center of your vision has the tightest packing; the edges trade resolution for sensitivity. This page explains why 20/20 vision means what it means, why the center of the night sky looks empty when you look directly at it, and why your eyes make tiny unconscious movements even when you try to hold still.

#### [[Wavelength Perception|Eye/Wavelength Perception]] — how light becomes color

Color vision rests on a simple idea: the eye does not have one sensor per color. It has **three overlapping types of cone** — broadly sensitive to short (blue-violet), medium (green), and long (red-orange) wavelengths. The brain reads color from the **relative balance** among those three signals, not from any single receptor on its own. That is why the same cone output could mean a different wavelength or a different brightness — and why comparing the three channels is what creates hue. If your main question is "how does the eye turn rainbow light into red, green, and blue experience?", start here.

#### [[Opponent-Process Color Coding|Eye/Opponent-Process Color Coding]] — how the brain repackages color signals

After the cones fire, the visual system **re-encodes** color into opposing pairs: red versus green, blue versus yellow, light versus dark. This is why you never see a reddish-green, and why staring at a strong color leaves a ghost of its opposite on a white wall (**afterimages**). The cone stage asks "how much of each wavelength band?"; the opponent stage asks "which direction on the warm–cool and light–dark axes?" — two layers that work together, not two competing theories.

### How the pieces fit together

These pages follow the path of light in order: **Anatomy** (where it goes) → **Rods vs. Cones** (which detectors do what, and where) → **Visual Acuity** (how sharp vision can get) → **Wavelength Perception** (three cones → color from ratios) → **Opponent-Process** (brain repackaging and perceptual effects). None of them stands alone — acuity depends on rod/cone layout, color depends on cone ratios, and afterimages depend on opponent channels.

So if you are visiting for the first time: start with **[[Anatomy|Eye/Anatomy]]** if you want the physical tour of the eye, or **[[Wavelength Perception|Eye/Wavelength Perception]]** if you mainly want to know how light becomes color — then read the others in whatever order your curiosity pulls you.
