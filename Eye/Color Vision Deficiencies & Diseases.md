**Scope:** How color vision deficiencies and eye diseases — "nature's experiments" — reveal the biological machinery of normal color perception: the three cone types, the opponent channels, and the cortical construction of color.

Every human visual system is an experiment of nature, and every deficiency or disease of the eye is a perturbation of that experiment that teaches us what the normal mechanism does. When a cone type is missing, a channel is compromised, or a cortical area is destroyed, the symptom pattern maps directly onto the biology: **trichromacy, opponent channels, and the cortex each leave their own signature in how color fails.**

### The Normal Baseline: Trichromacy

Human color vision is trichromatic: three cone classes in the retina, each sensitive to a different region of the spectrum:

- **S cones:** peak ~440 nm (blue-violet); rare — only **5–10%** (roughly **6.5%**) of the cone population.
- **M cones:** peak ~530 nm (green).
- **L cones:** peak ~560 nm (yellow-green; historically called "red").
- The **L:M:S ratio is approximately 10:5:1**, and the ~6 million cones concentrate in the fovea — the foundation of the [[Rods vs. Cones - Density & Distribution|cone mosaic]] described in the Eye section.

### Congenital Deficiencies: When a Cone Is Missing or Shifted

Congenital color vision deficiencies are hereditary and present from birth. They come in two families:

- **Anomalous trichromacy** — all three cones are present, but one has a shifted spectral sensitivity: **protanomaly** (L shifted), **deuteranomaly** (M shifted), **tritanomaly** (S shifted). The retina still has three channels, but the ratios between them are altered, so hue discrimination is weakened rather than abolished. Anomalous trichromacy highlights how enormously the L and M curves overlap: even a small shift in one curve measurably changes the "ratio" used to identify hues.
- **Dichromacy** — one cone type is missing or non-functional: **protanopia** (L missing or replaced by M pigment), **deuteranopia** (M missing or replaced by L pigment), **tritanopia** (S missing). Only two cone types remain, and one entire opponent channel is typically compromised — see [[Opponent-Process Color Coding|opponent-process coding]] for why that channel matters.

**Genetics and prevalence:**

- Red-green deficiencies (protan and deutan) affect roughly **7–8% of men but under 1% of women** (less than 0.5% in some estimates). The L and M opsin genes sit on the **X chromosome**; men have one X, so a single defective gene expresses, while women need two.
- The S-cone opsin gene is **autosomal**, so tritanopia is extremely rare (~**0.005%** of men; under ~0.02% overall). Blue-yellow deficiency is simply harder to inherit.
- The same X-linked L/M arrangement that produces deficiency in men can produce **tetrachromacy** in some women — carriers with two different L/M opsin variants and four functional cone classes.

**Neutral points:** every dichromat has a wavelength that appears colorless gray — where the two surviving cone types give equal responses. Protanopes see gray at **~492 nm**, deuteranopes at **~498 nm**, tritanopes at **~570 nm**. These numbers are fingerprints of the surviving cones' sensitivity curves.

### Monochromacy: The World in Grays

When color fails entirely, the world is a grayscale — and monochromacy is the strongest proof of **univariance** (a single receptor type cannot distinguish wavelength from intensity; only comparison across types can):

- **Rod monochromacy (achromatopsia):** total lack of cone function. Rods are hypersensitive and saturate quickly, so these individuals suffer **day blindness** (photophobia) and very low visual acuity, with the world in shades of gray.
- **Cone monochromacy:** only one cone type functions. Color is still absent, but daytime acuity is much better than in rod monochromacy.

Because monochromats can match any colored light with any other by adjusting only intensity, they demonstrate that without at least two receptor types, wavelength information is simply not recoverable.

### Acquired Deficiencies: When Disease Damages the Path

Unlike congenital conditions, acquired deficiencies appear later in life, can affect only one eye, and damage the *path* rather than the inherited *receptors*:

- **Cataract:** clouding and **yellowing of the lens** acts as a blue-absorbing filter, producing an acquired **blue-yellow deficiency** — patients struggle to distinguish blue from green, and green from yellow. (Goethe already described the odd optics of a "milky cataract": a turbid lens can make a patient see red light.) The aging lens yellowing is universal: a 1984 study found seniors had difficulty differentiating blue/green and green/yellow capsules.
- **Age-related macular degeneration (AMD):** progressive loss of cones in the **fovea** — the region of sharpest color vision (see [[Visual Acuity & Receptor Spacing|acuity and receptor spacing]]).
- **Glaucoma, diabetic retinopathy, optic neuritis:** damage to the retinal ganglion cells or optic nerve, which interrupts the opponent signals leaving the eye.

A striking regularity: **acquired losses often follow a tritan-like (blue-yellow) pattern**, whereas congenital losses are predominantly red-green — evidence that the blue-yellow channel is more fragile along the disease pathway, while the red-green channel is the one that breaks genetically.

### Cortical Achromatopsia: The Eye Sees, the Brain Doesn't

The most surgical of nature's experiments: damage to the **V4 color area** (the hV4 cluster and anterior VO1/VO2 regions of the ventral cortex), often from a stroke. The cones and retinal opponent signals remain **perfectly healthy** — but the brain can no longer process them into color experience. Patients know that a banana *is* yellow from memory, yet cannot select the correct color crayon and see no hue anywhere. This is the decisive evidence that **color perception is a high-level cortical construction**, not a retina readout — and it localizes that construction to V4.

### What Deficiencies Reveal About Normal Vision

- **Univariance is mandatory:** monochromacy proves a single receptor class cannot see color; comparison between types is required.
- **Opponent channels fail in pairs:** color blindness patterns show color lost as red-green or blue-yellow *together*, confirming the antagonistic channels of [[Opponent-Process Color Coding|opponent-process theory]] as real mechanisms, not metaphors.
- **L/M overlap defines normal discrimination:** anomalous trichromacy shows how heavily normal hue discrimination leans on the fine balance of two enormously overlapping curves.
- **S-cone scarcity is an optimization:** the rarity of tritan defects and the scarcity of S cones reflect a system tuned for L/M luminance and spatial detail, with short-wavelength information de-emphasized — even in normal observers (see below).
- **Color is cortical:** cortical achromatopsia separates healthy receptors from destroyed experience, proving the felt quality of color arises in the brain.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's color temperature studies add the sobering caveat that **normal trichromats are not always trichromats**. The S-cone contribution above ~570 nm ("greenish yellow") is less than 0.01% of peak — so in the yellow-to-red wavelengths only the L and M cones can discriminate spectral hues, and under ordinary luminance contrast the tiny S-cone signal drops below threshold. Our eyes become "**functionally dichromatic**" in the long-wavelength range; MacEvoy concludes that *brown* — a color that has no pure spectral wavelength — "signals a contrast induced colorblindness (tritanopia) in normal vision." Dichromacy research is therefore not a study of rare freaks; it is the study of a limit that normal vision itself touches every day in the warm part of the spectrum *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: "Color Blind" Is Not a Binary Category**
> Popular teaching sorts people into "normal trichromats" and the ~8% of men who are "color blind" — but the deficiency spectrum is continuous, and the boundary is not where the anatomy says. MacEvoy's data show that even confirmed trichromats are functionally dichromatic (tritanopic) in the yellow-red range, because the S-cone signal there is negligible — brown itself is the perceptual signature of that induced dichromacy *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]])*. Meanwhile anomalous trichromats — who outnumber dichromats and retain three channels — fall on a graded scale of discrimination loss rather than a categorical defect. "Color blind" is a spectrum, not a switch.
