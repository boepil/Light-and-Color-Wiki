## Synthesized Content

Color vision is founded on the **Trichromatic Theory**, which posits that the retina uses three distinct types of cone photoreceptors sensitive to different portions of the spectrum (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). While roughly 120 million **rods** operate in low light for monochromatic night vision, approximately 6 to 7 million **cones** mediate color perception in daylight (**Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

*   **Short-wavelength (S) cones** are sensitive to blue-violet light, with peak sensitivity at approximately 440–448 nm (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). They are the rarest type, making up only 5–10% of the cone population (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
*   **Medium-wavelength (M) cones** peak in the green region at approximately 518–540 nm (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
*   **Long-wavelength (L) cones** are sensitive to red-orange light, peaking at approximately 560–617 nm (**Contemporary Color**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

Each cone follows the **principle of univariance**, meaning a single receptor's output is a scalar value that cannot distinguish between changes in wavelength and changes in light intensity (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). The brain determines color by comparing the relative patterns of activation across these three overlapping cone systems (**The Science of Paintings**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
