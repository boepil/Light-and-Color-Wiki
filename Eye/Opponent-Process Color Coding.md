**Scope:** How the eye and brain turn three raw cone signals into color — the opponent re-encoding that explains unique hues, afterimages, and "impossible" colors.

When does a wave of light become a color? There is no single instant. Color is built up gradually, stage by stage, and each stage makes the signal less like a raw physical measurement and more like a stable, contextualized category. The story has three acts: the **retina measures** (the trichromatic cones of [[Wavelength Perception|Wavelength Perception]]), the **eye and brain re-encode** (opponent channels — this page), and the **cortex interprets** (see [[The Neuroscience Behind Why Colours Rewire Your Brain]]). This second stage was first formally proposed in **1878** by the German physiologist **Ewald Hering**, and it explains why we never perceive "reddish-green," why complementary **[[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing|afterimages]]** occur, and why complementary pigments cancel to gray instead of making a third hue.

### 1. Stage One: A Single Cone Cannot See Color

A single cone's output is a single scalar value that confounds two physical variables: the **intensity** of the light and its **wavelength**. This is the **principle of univariance** — a dim light near a cone's peak sensitivity can produce the identical neural signal as a much brighter light at an off-peak wavelength, because the response depends on how many photons are absorbed, not on what wavelength they are.

- A single receptor type is effectively **color blind**; wavelength information can only be recovered by **comparing cone types against one another**.
- So the direct answer to "does color come out of a cone?" is **no** — nothing leaving a photoreceptor is color yet. The comparison is what first makes wavelength recoverable at all, and that comparison happens in the next layer of retina.

### 2. The Retina Re-encodes: Antagonistic Circuits

The comparison is performed by **antagonistic wiring** — the center-surround circuits of **horizontal, bipolar, and ganglion cells**. In these circuits one cone type provides an excitatory ("plus") input while another cone type provides an inhibitory ("minus") input to the same neuron:

- The four chromatically opponent receptive-field types are **R+G−, G+R−, B+Y−, and Y+B−** — each cell reports a *difference*, not a raw cone readout.
- The mechanism behaves like a **teeter-totter**: when the L and M inputs are equal, the signal is balanced (seen as neutral gray); when one side dominates, the balance tilts toward a specific color.
- Each cone's raw signal is therefore wired into two opposing circuits at once — excitation from one cone type, inhibition from another. **That antagonism, not a color signal, is what leaves the retina.**

### 3. Stage Two: Measuring the Opponent Channels

![[images/Pasted image 20260814191059.png]]


The second figure lays the three measured channels out as **algebraic comparisons between cone signals** — the exact form in which the corpus's own wiring diagram presents them (Krauskopf's Fig. 3.12; Palmer pp. 113–114):

- **Red–green channel: L − M** — when L-cone activity exceeds M, the balance tilts toward red; when M exceeds L, toward green. There is no separate "red receptor" or "green receptor" at this stage.
- **Blue–yellow channel: S − (L + M)** — S-cone dominance drives the signal toward blue; combined L+M dominance drives it toward yellow. **Yellow has no receptor of its own** — it is an opponent percept generated by this comparison.
- **Luminance channel: L + M** — the roughly achromatic *sum* that carries brightness, shape, and detail.

Note the diagram's label "Color Opponent Cortex" is abbreviated/mistyped — this stage begins in the **retina** (horizontal, bipolar, and ganglion cells), not the cortex; the cortex stages beyond it are covered in section 7. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*

In the **1950s, Hurvich and Jameson** turned this qualitative theory into measured functions with **hue-cancellation experiments**: observers added, say, green light until it exactly canceled the redness of a test light, wavelength by wavelength. The amount of canceling light required traces the channel's true response curve.

- Three opponent channels were measured: **Red–Green, Blue–Yellow, and Black–White (luminance)**.
- Each curve **crosses zero at specific wavelengths** — the neutral points where the channel signals no color. For example, the red–green channel sits at zero near **~500 nm**, where a light appears pure green, and the blue–yellow channel's neutral point occurs near **~570 nm**.
- These are not metaphors: they are close to what Hurvich and Jameson actually measured in humans.

### 4. Why Re-encode at All? Redundancy and Economy

The L and M cone response curves overlap enormously — most light activates them almost identically — so two separate L and M channels would be **mostly redundant**. The antagonistic wiring removes that redundancy:

- One channel carries the **difference** (chromatic information), another the **sum** (luminance). Same total information, decorrelated — cheaper to carry, more useful downstream.
- The split also separates **illumination changes** (shadows, which change luminance) from **surface reflectance changes** (which change chromaticity) — the separation that higher-level color constancy later exploits.

### 5. What the Wiring Explains

The antagonistic structure is the actual mechanism behind three everyday observations:

- **Impossible colors:** you can never perceive "reddish-green" or "bluish-yellow," because a single neural channel cannot be positive and negative at once.
- **Afterimages:** staring at a saturated color fatigues one half of an opponent pair; shifting to neutral white — which normally drives both sides equally — lets the non-fatigued half temporarily dominate. Staring at red thus produces a **green afterimage** (empirically often **cyan**, consistent with an RGB-filtering model of the neural substrate).
- **Complementary cancellation:** complementary pigments cancel toward gray rather than producing a third hue — the mixing consequence of the same opponent balance.

### 6. No New Information, But New Structure

The re-encoding is **mathematically reversible** — the original L, M, and S signals can be reconstructed from the opponent channels, so no new information is created. But it creates new *perceptual structure*:

- **Red, green, blue, and yellow feel like elemental, unmixed colors** — Hering's four "unique hues" — because they correspond to the **zero-crossings** of the opponent curves.
- **Orange feels like an obvious mixture** of red and yellow, because it requires activation across multiple channels. That categorical feel comes from where the curves cross zero, not from anything in the raw cone data.

### 7. Beyond the Retina: From Signal to Object

The opponent signals travel up a hierarchy, each stage adding context:

- **LGN (lateral geniculate nucleus):** mostly a relay with **gain control**, keeping color and luminance in separate organized layers (parvocellular vs. magnocellular).
- **V1 (primary visual cortex):** **double-opponent cells** in the cytochrome-oxidase "blobs" combine color with **spatial information** — edges, orientation, texture.
- **V2:** adds **boundary and figure-ground** processing.
- **V4 (hV4):** the site of **color constancy** — a banana reads as "yellow" under blue-tinted daylight *and* orange-tinted tungsten because V4 actively corrects for the light source rather than reporting the raw signal. Damage here causes **achromatopsia**: healthy cones and opponent signals, but the world seen in grays.
- **Higher still:** color is reparameterized into **hue/saturation polar coordinates** (in the V4/ventral-occipital cluster) and **categorically clustered** — fMRI shows named colors pulled together in the neural map while category boundaries are pushed apart. The exact "hue and saturation cells" remain hypothesized, not yet found.

### 8. The Honest Limit: When Does a Wave Become Color?

There is no single instant — it is a **gradual re-representation**, each stage making the signal less "raw measurement" and more "stable, contextualized category." Neuroscience can describe this chain in detail through V4 and beyond. What it **cannot** explain is why any of that processing is accompanied by an actual felt experience — why there is *something it is like* to see blue, rather than the computation just happening in the dark. That is the **hard problem of consciousness**, genuinely unresolved.

### Modern Legacy: Opponent Axes in Colorimetry

- The stage logic of opponent processing is the explicit basis of **[[Why Lab-Munsell Were Built for Perceptual Uniformity|CIELAB]]** (1976): the a* axis is red–green, b* is blue–yellow, and the L* lightness axis plays the white–black channel.
- The **[[Munsell Notation|Munsell]]** system orders color by the visual dimensions of equal-appearing differences — hue, value, chroma — a perceptual ordering strongly influenced by Hering's four psychological primaries.
- Modern **color appearance models** (e.g., CIECAM97) retain opponent axes plus adaptation states; the blue–yellow channel follows a curved function that coincides with the **daylight locus**, aiding color constancy — and the blue–yellow system appears evolutionarily older than red–green.

### Concrete Data

- **Cone ratios:** S-cones are only ~5–10% (~6.5%) of the cone population; the L:M:S ratio is roughly **10:5:1**. S-cones are absent from the central ~0.1° of the fovea.
- **Peak sensitivities:** ~440 nm (S), ~530 nm (M), ~560 nm (L).
- **Ganglion cell proportions:** parvocellular (P) cells — the chromatic pathway — outnumber magnocellular (M) cells by roughly **8–10×**.
- **Luminous efficiency:** the photopic luminosity function V(λ), which drives the achromatic channel, peaks near **555 nm** in the green-yellow region — the same asymmetry that makes green pigments reach far higher chroma than reds (see [[Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red]]).
- **Neutral points:** dichromats show wavelengths where they see only gray: **492 nm** for protanopes, **498 nm** for deuteranopes.
- **Receptive field size** increases hierarchically from **0.1–0.5°** in area V1 to **25° or more** in the inferotemporal (IT) cortex.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's color theory pages treat the opponent dimensions as the modern working framework: "the a+/a− and b+/b− opponent dimensions of modern perceptual color spaces are the most effective framework for analyzing complementary color relationships," with the four Hering unique hues marking where the opponent functions cross zero in the measured wheel. But he draws a hard line between *neural* antagonism and *paint* antagonism: "mix to gray" complementarity is not proof of visual harmony, "mixture antagonism does not define visual contrast," and what matters to a viewer is the finished color relationship on the canvas, not the mixing chemistry that produced it *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: Visual vs. Mixing Complements**
> Handprint vehemently warns against a major fallacy in traditional color theory: conflating these *visual* opponent complements with physical *mixing* complements. While opponent-process theory dictates that green and magenta are visual opposites, mixing green and magenta paints does not yield a neutral gray due to physical reflectance overlap. This crucial distinction is expanded on in [[Perceptual Complements vs. Mixing Complements]]. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*
