**Scope:** The Bauhaus and its color pedagogy — the Vorkurs, Itten's seven contrasts and color sphere, Kandinsky's synesthetic color-music, Klee's keystone gray, and Albers's experimental divergence.

### Color as an Autonomous Formal Element

At the Bauhaus color was treated as an **autonomous formal element** interacting dynamically with form and human perception, taught through the **Vorkurs (preliminary course)** — originally aimed at investigating color "as an independent element in a 'vacuum', stripped of its traditional associations with imagery or function". Bleicher rates the school "probably the most influential of all of the various groups on contemporary art and design".

### Johannes Itten's Pedagogy

- **The seven contrasts:** hue, light-dark, cold-warm, complementary, simultaneous, saturation, and extension.
- **12-hue color circle** and a **color sphere** — "a symmetric solid adopted from Philipp Otto Runge" — to visualize the totality of the color universe.
- **Subjective color** ("subjective timbre"): every student "possesses a personal, inherent preference for specific color combinations that serve as a key to their individual mode of thinking and feeling".
- **Spiritual framing:** while objective principles exist, the "deepest and truest secrets" of color are "beheld by the heart alone".

### Wassily Kandinsky's Doctrine

- **Synesthetic color-music:** color as a universal "language of the soul" bypassing cultural associations, with fixed **color–form coordinates**: **yellow = triangle** (aggressive/earthly), **red = square** (stable/matter), **blue = circle** (spiritual/concentric).
- ***On the Spiritual in Art:*** colors set up "vibrations" in the soul, hues acting like musical instruments — vermilion red as "loud beats of the drum".
- Labeled a **"pseudo-synaesthete"**: "impossible to verify if his multiple sensory responses were truly involuntary or artistically constructed".

### Paul Klee's Approach

- Color as **melody and movement**, with an "uncommon breadth" of color resources that avoided reduction to a single expressive character.
- While Itten ran the Vorkurs, Klee lectured on the **"problems of form"**; his teaching notes (including the **Wassily album**) treated the color wheel and its **middle-point gray** as the "neutral center" or balancing **"keystone"** of the whole system, "where all color tensions find equilibrium".

### Josef Albers's Divergence

- Itten's former student, Albers "moved away from mystical systems toward a focus on the **perceptual relativity of color**".
- ***Interaction of Color:*** "in visual perception a color is almost never seen as it really is"; "**color deceives continually**"; color is "the most relative medium in art," its "action" depending entirely on its neighbors.
- **Method:** to eliminate "self-expression" and the physical handling of paint, Albers's exercises used **pre-colored paper chips (color-aid papers)** and silk-screened plates to demonstrate how one hue can appear radically different on different grounds.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's verdict on the Bauhaus theorists runs on three threads. First, the **hue-only shorthand**: when the "color crank Johannes Itten announces that 'yellow against purple provides a very large contrast between light and dark'" he is visualizing saturated hues alone, ignoring that dark valued yellows are blackish and light valued purples are whitish — the classic traditional-color-theory equation of "color" with saturated hue. Second, **Albers as the exception**: traditional color theory "has almost entirely ignored spatial effects in visual color — the studies of simulated transparency by Joseph Albers being one of the notable exceptions," and the genre-defining "squares within squares" format (a ≥1° square inside a ≥3× field) descends from Chevreul through the post-Bauhaus tradition to Albers's iconic paintings. Third, the **universal-psychology debunk**: Bauhaus designers themselves sent out survey questionnaires seeking consistent emotional color associations, with "hundreds of publications" showing none — "chromatic Esperanto" that cultures reject; Kandinsky's red was "spiritual" only because of the red-cornered icon rooms of rural Russia. Itten's and Kandinsky's "spiritual or 'moral' qualities" attributed to primaries figure among the claims MacEvoy dismisses as "misleading generalizations or crackpot nonsense" *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs supplies the documentary history behind Itten's pedagogy — and the sharpest indictment of its scientific status:

- **The Bauhaus color course was largely Hoelzel's, filtered through Itten.** Colour was taught at the Bauhaus by Itten, Ludwig Hirschfeld-Mack, Paul Klee, and Wassily Kandinsky, but "the dominant influence on colour theory initially was the former mentor/teacher of Itten and Hirschfeld-Mack, the painter Adolf Hoelzel (1853-1934), who also devised virtually the entire structure of the Preliminary Course." Hoelzel's approach was relatively broad — it "attached prime importance to Goethe," but incorporated von Bezold, Ogden Rood and Wilhelm Ostwald as well, and used different colour diagrams for different purposes, including a `circle divided into six unequal hue sectors calculated from the ratios of the 'proportional activity of the retina'` that **Schopenhauer** had asserted — six of Hoelzel's diagrams were printed in a 1919 Pelikan publication *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*.
- **Itten took Hoelzel's diagrams — and then regressed.** His 1921 "colour star" lithograph "follows Hoelzel's 12-hue system derived from Bezold," and in *The Art of Color* he even "appropriated some of Hoelzel's diagrams, including the circle based on Schopenhauer's ratios, which Itten mistakenly thought were Goethe's." But the 1961 book "reverts to a system comprising evenly-spaced historical primaries and secondaries, with six intermediates" (flipping the hue sequence vertically, primary yellow at top centre) — "post-Bauhaus version of Itten's star is thus only a variation on its 19th century forerunners such as that of Lacouture." For a three-dimensional framework he rejected Ostwald and "simply ignored the Munsell system," reverting to a simple sphere "externally like that of Runge (1810)," whose equator puts "the strongest colours of all hues... ignoring their different value and absolute chroma" — a model that "lacks a consistent representation of the dimension of value that is vital to most painters" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/083.md|083.html]])*.
- **The primaries are defined in the vocabulary of the science the book otherwise ignores.** Itten's three primaries are "defined in the text as perceptually pure hues, oddly enough using the names of the **four** psychological primaries of modern science, which are otherwise completely ignored in the book": "a red that is neither bluish nor yellowish; a yellow that is neither greenish nor reddish; and a blue that is neither greenish nor reddish." His secondaries are claimed "to be also exactly complementary to the third primary in paint-mixing, in simultaneous and successive contrast, and even in expressive interpretation." Because the system is "structured symmetrically around just three of the four psychological primaries," his hue circle is "uneven perceptually, with relatively large hue steps in the yellow-green-blue sector" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]])*.
- **His mixing theory was obsolete before the Bauhaus existed.** Itten's accounts of successive and simultaneous contrast "echo Goethe's invocation of a mysterious 'eye animism'... completely ignoring the hypothesis of photoreceptor adaptation already suggested by Palmer and Young"; his discussion of mix in paints is framed by the intermixture model "that had been rendered finally obsolete by Helmholtz"; "no mention is made of the subtractive primaries cyan and magenta," and his primaries are so far from ideal that "paints of these hues make a poor set of colourant-mixing primaries, quite unsuitable for mixing purples or strong middle greens." Nor do the claimed complementaries hold: the accurate afterimage complement of a middle (5R) red is bluish-green ("about 5BG"), not Itten's middle green *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]])*.
- **The verdict is the page's most-quoted line.** *The Art of Color* "embodies an approach to colour theory and classification fixated at a stage at least seventy years before the founding of the Bauhaus" and "in several ways it is the colour theory equivalent of so-called 'creation science'." Briggs's closing judgment ties the pedagogy directly to its persistence: "the teaching in many institutions of a near monoculture of Itten's simplistic post-Bauhaus colour theory has been disastrous for the general level of public understanding of colour today" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]])*.
