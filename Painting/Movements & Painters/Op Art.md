**Scope:** Op Art — Vasarely and Riley's precise geometries and high-contrast patterns that turn retinal fatigue, simultaneous contrast, and saccades into the medium itself.

### Retinal Effects and Mechanisms

Op Art uses "precise geometries and high-contrast patterns to exploit the physiological limitations and 'inventiveness' of the human visual system" — creating illusions of movement, depth, and "supplementary" color on flat surfaces.

- **Vibrating boundaries:** when "two colors of contrasting hues but similar light intensities" meet, the "aggressive" flickering of "enforced contours" feels uncomfortable because the visual system struggles to localize the edge.
- **Moiré and motion illusions:** repetitive geometries interact with **saccadic eye movements** to create retinal displacement — ring-shaped shadows and shimmering "**ring of Kitaoka**" patterns.
- **"Snake" patterns / peripheral drift:** in Akiyoshi Kitaoka's *Rotating Snakes*, brightness gradients (black→gray, white→gray) "act as a 'motor' for apparent motion" — different perceived speeds across depth layers make the snakes "roll" in peripheral vision.
- **Ouchi illusion:** contrasting vertical/horizontal stripe fields appear to move in opposite directions as the viewer shifts, a "keyhole effect" of spatial depth.

### Bridget Riley: Method and Works

- **Method of instability:** a "formal backbone" (drawing) grounds the "instability of color relationships"; simple repeated stripes/curves act as "neutral vehicles" letting "color energies develop uninhibitedly".
- Goal: **supplementary colors** that "hover in a film in front of the canvas through the viewer's eye activity".
- **Works:** *Current* (1964) — black-and-white patterns in small units inducing hue where "only black and white physically exist" (the **Benham effect**); *Blaze* and *Fall* — early-60s monochromatic "warped spatial planes" and "pulsating" patterns; *Winter Palace* (1981) — repeated neutral shapes at massive scale producing "a mysterious film of mixed color"; *Song of Orpheus 5* (1978) — labile non-primary colors (turquoise, olive) that shift and "join" through eye activity.

### Relation to Eye Fatigue and Simultaneous Contrast

- **Retinal fatigue:** staring at high-contrast patterns overstimulates photoreceptors; when the gaze shifts, "tired cells fire less strongly," producing **negative afterimages in the complement**.
- **[[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing|Simultaneous contrast]]:** the eye "spontaneously generates the complement of any color it sees to restore equilibrium"; Op Art places colors together to incite this "active relatedness" — a "dark light" or "vibrational intensity" created in the viewer's mind, "not objectively present in the pigment".
- **Durational viewing:** Op Art paintings are "durational experiences" — "the visual organization of the image literally changes over time," "shifting from a flat surface to a warped spatial plane".

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's diagnosis of why traditional color theory ignores such work: "Traditional color theory has almost entirely ignored spatial effects in visual color — the studies of simulated transparency by Joseph Albers being one of the notable exceptions — but these effects are incredibly large." Op Art is the pure demonstration of that magnitude, and MacEvoy's scaling observations explain the craft: in the Chevreul-format demonstrations of contrast, "the apparent shift is weaker in this format, as the larger color areas are able to stand on their own" — which is precisely why Riley's early works break fields into small units where induced effects survive, and why the "enforced contour" of equal-intensity hues is the perceptual engine of vibrating boundaries rather than any doctrine of color harmony *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*.
