**Scope:** Post-Impressionism — how Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin, and Seurat lifted color from the Impressionist recording of light into expressive, structural, and scientific systems.

### The Post-Impressionist Shift

Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin, and Seurat "shifted the focus of art from the Impressionist recording of fleeting light to a deeper exploration of the expressive, structural, and scientific possibilities of color." Rooted in Impressionism's attachment to natural light, they "largely rejected its mimetic function" in favor of symbolic meaning and formal harmony (*A Comprehensive Overview...*, *Color in Art*, *Modern Art*).

### Vincent van Gogh: Emotional and Complementary Color

- Color as a **"language of the soul"** evoking psychological states; *The Bedroom* was meant "to rest the brain and imagination" through its color relationships (*Color in Art*, *Contemporary Color*).
- **Complementary pairs** as a "ray of light" comparable to physical laws like gravitation; in *Night (After Millet)* blue shadows complement orange firelight, following **Goethe's colored-shadow theory** (*Color in Art*).
- Enlarged Neo-Impressionism's **pointillé** dots into "long, rhythmic, cursive brushstrokes" to intensify color vibration (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Paul Cézanne: Structural Color and Temperature Modulations

- Made Impressionism "something solid and enduring" — **form built from warm-to-cool temperature modulations** instead of light and shade, an "ethereal effect" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*, *Contemporary Color*).
- **Passages:** color areas with distinct and indistinct boundaries as "plastic organization" creating spatial depth (*Color in Art*).
- Rejected the pointillist dot as "too static and mechanical," preferring rectangular **open brushstrokes** that integrate the whole picture both formally and chromatically (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Paul Gauguin: Symbolic Flat Color

- Color to call up "immediate sensations akin to music," described as "enigmatic" (*Contemporary Color*, *Color in Art*).
- **Japonisme:** broad flat, unmodeled color for a decorative, symbolic effect (*The Art of Color*, *Modern Art*).
- **Liberation from nature:** "if he wanted to paint a tree red to give it more power than it has in nature, he did so" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Contemporary Color*).

### Georges Seurat: Reconstituting Light Scientifically

- Self-described **"impressioniste-luministe"** seeking to "reconstitute light" through color — organizing "random" color into an objective whole (*A Comprehensive Overview...*, *The Science of Paintings*).
- **Pointillism/optical mixture:** dots of pure color merging in the eye, "theoretically creating greater luminosity than palette mixing" (*The Science of Paintings*).
- Grounded in **Chevreul's Law of Simultaneous Contrast** and **Ogden Rood's** spinning-disc studies of visual mixing (*The Art of Color*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### The Reaction Against Impressionism

- Beyond the "fleeting moment" to "permanent principles of form and order" (Cézanne, Seurat) (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Subjectivity over mimicry:** expressive and symbolic function over objective local color (*Contemporary Color*, *Color in Art*).
- **Flatness:** the canvas as "a plane surface covered with colors" — the realization that "paved the way for modern abstraction and movements like Fauvism and Cubism" (*Color in Art*, *Modern Art*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's materials-eye view of the group is threefold. First, **palette fingerprints**: artists "tend to have a distinctive preference in warm paints" — Caravaggio, Turner, Gauguin, and Matisse "seem to like red orange focal hues, while others (Van Gogh, Rubens, Rembrandt) seem to prefer a deep yellow." Second, **hue-to-value discipline**: the "clearest mapping of hue to value" appears in "medieval and certain modernist painting styles (late Van Gogh, Matisse, Derain, Ellsworth Kelly, etc.), where pure pigment colors are used much more than mixed colors or colors darkened with black" — dark values cannot be pure saturated green or orange without dulling to brown. Third, **Gauguin's "warm yellow light" scheme**: sampling 250 pixels from a Gauguin canvas, MacEvoy shows the palette map — nine or more paints — arranged as a "huge yellow ochre cluster" with "accenting blues and greens at the saturated limits," imitating a deep yellow illuminant and eliminating the magenta-violet sector to intensify scarlet; a "careful limitations and accents" imitation of warm light, not arbitrary color. And on permanence: Van Gogh was "maddeningly slipshod" in his use of painting materials, in the company of Reynolds and Whistler *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/tech13.md|tech13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]])*.
