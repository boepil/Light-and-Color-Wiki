**Scope:** Turner and Romanticism's revolutionary color — the atmospheric veil, the controversion of the warm-tone academic doctrine, the Newton/Goethe engagement, and the late dissolution of form into light.

### "Psychico-Expressive" Color and the Atmospheric Veil

In Turner's work color shifted "from a tool for description to an autonomous, 'psychico-expressive' medium used to evoke internal atmospheric mood and spiritual depth" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview of Color Vision Mechanisms, Color Spaces, and the Munsell System*).

- The **atmospheric veil** treats air as "a series of thin 'curtains' or films intervening between the eye and the horizon": as objects recede they are seen through more veils, "becoming cooler in color and lighter in value" (*Carlson's Guide to Landscape Painting*).
- Turner applied the principle radically — "delicately graduated cool tones (blues, whites, and pale greens)" even for warm subjects like fire, as in *The Burning of the Houses of Lords and Commons* (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Controverting the "Doctrine of the Balance of Colors"

Turner was the primary figure in abandoning the academic **"doctrine of the balance of colors"** — the rule that paintings should carry predominantly warm tonality, "a belief reinforced by the look of old, yellowed varnish" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

- He demonstrated that a successful composition "could be built from cool colors supported by a single 'point of rich brownish crimson'" instead of warm-dominant masses (*Color in Art*).
- His **unbalanced area distributions** and high-contrast temperature shifts created "an 'expressionistic intensity' that bypassed academic requirements for static equilibrium" (*Color in Art*, *Contemporary Color*).

### Turner and the Science of His Day

Turner's palette "acted as a 'sounding board' for the scientific and intellectual interests of his circle" (*Color in Art*). He engaged the era's central conflict: **Newton's spectral analysis** (light as a mixture of colors, pure physical decomposition) versus **Goethe's Theory of Colours** (subjective perception, colored shadows) — the Goethean side deeply informed the Romantic interest in "how light hits the eye" (*Color in Art*, *Theory of Colours*). Practically, "the most brilliant effects occur in dense atmospheres where particles afford 'prismatic refraction'" — a phenomenon Turner mastered "in his depiction of light filtering through fog and mist" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### The Late Works: Toward Abstraction

In his late period Turner pushed color out of its "imprisonment in the world of object," treating **light itself as the primary subject** (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

- The **sun paintings** ("Circular Forms" and sun-focused works) treated the sun not as an object but as "pure painting," using color to create "movement and vibration."
- In *Rain, Steam and Speed* form is "almost entirely dissolved into light and color," and the non-objective color compositions mark Turner as "often regarded as the first 'abstractionist' in European painting" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Romanticism's Rejections

- **Drawing vs. color:** rejection of the academic *disegno* hierarchy — that drawing was the basis of painting while color was "merely ornamental" or "merely an aid" to realism (*Color in Art*).
- **Local color:** rejected in favor of **optical color**, which "shifts constantly according to atmospheric conditions and the quality of light" (*Color in Art*, *Contemporary Color*).
- **Academic practice:** abandonment of "smooth application" and "fixed rules" for "fresh vision, unmediated perception, and the direct expression of emotion" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*, *Contemporary Color*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's painterly verdict on Turner is double: as a **color-materials man** — one of "the painters I most admire" for "incredible sensitivity to and masterful handling of the color materials, the paints themselves" — and as a **methodological heir on the value side**: Turner "described a very similar color wheel" to MacEvoy's own artist's value wheel "in his Royal Academy lectures on perspective" (per Martin Kemp's *The Science of Art*), the wheel being built on the yellow/blue (b\*) and light/dark (value) dimensions of CIELAB. MacEvoy also records Turner's notorious disregard for permanence — the manufacturer William Winsor chiding him for buying fugitive pigments, to which Turner replied, "It's your business to make paints, it's my business to use them" — the case that later forced the 1888 Russell–Abney *Report on the Action of Light on Water Colours* and the modern lightfastness standards *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color11.md|color11.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]])*.
