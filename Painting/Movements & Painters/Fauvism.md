### "Wild Beasts" and the Liberation of Color

The movement earned its name at the **1905 Salon d'Automne** in Paris, where a critic, startled by the group's aggressive color, branded them **"fauves"** — French for "wild beasts" (*Contemporary Color*, *Modern Art: A Very Short Introduction*). Their program: **liberate color from reality**, reacting sharply against **local color** — "the literal appearance of an object." The artist could "paint a tree blue or red to give the object an intensity and power it would not possess in nature" (*Contemporary Color*, *A Comprehensive Overview of Color Vision Mechanisms, Color Spaces, and the Munsell System*).

### Stylistic Hallmarks

- **Pure high-chroma color** in flat areas and broad brushstrokes; **negation of modeling** in favor of two-dimensional flatness (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Art of Color*).
- **The "Green Line":** Matisse's 1905 portrait *Madame Matisse* runs a light green line down the face's center, dividing it "into a hemisphere of 'harsh yellows' and one of natural ochre" (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Vibrational complements:** Matisse paired a **violet eyebrow** against a dominant yellow field to heighten intensity through simultaneous contrast (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Contemporary Color*).
- **Color-drawing fusion:** "color and drawing became inextricably linked" — blue arabesques in *Harmony in Red* define form and space while insisting on flatness (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Matisse's Philosophy of Color

- **Expression over representation:** color should "do everything," be "suggestive of mood rather than a 'representational element'" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Art of Color*).
- **Subjective equilibrium:** compositions balance through "simple, luminous areas" — rationally calculated beforehand, with sketches and written color notes, "only to modify them later based on his subjective feeling during the process" (*Contemporary Color*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **The flatness motto:** "**Plus c'est plat, plus c'est de l'art**" — "The flatter it is, the more it is art" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Ancestry and the Shift to Cubism

- Fauvism grew out of **Van Gogh, Gauguin, and Bonnard**, continuing Gauguin's experiments with large color areas and the flat, unmodeled symbolic style of Japanese prints (cloisonnism) (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Art of Color*).
- By breaking naturalism entirely, Fauvism "paved the way for the move toward abstraction and the development of Cubism"; Georges Braque moved from Fauvism to Cubism, where the "polychromatic wealth" of Fauve color was replaced by a somber palette of ochre, brown, and blue to focus on geometric form (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Modern Art*).

### Critical Reaction

The initial response was shock and ridicule — the "fauve" label born of "a perceived lack of discipline and a departure from the 'smooth application' of traditional academic art" (*Contemporary Color*, *Color in Art*). The movement is now recognized as foundational to modernism, shifting painting's "focus from mimicry to the witnessed performance of the artist's expressive manipulation of paint" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's history places Fauvism inside the Newtonian "emancipation of color": movements like the Fauves used "shocking" or "forbidden" or "wild" color schemes "to break out of conventional color codes and use color to say new things" — and then, "after a brief period of argumentative turmoil, these practices became the new color norms, the new color codes, and color dogma rolled on as complacently as before." His separate point for painters: the *green line* and violet-yellow pairings are not arbitrariness but disciplined use of complementary contrast on closely matched values, of a piece with his charge that the great colorists managed "color materials" rather than doctrines *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]])*.
