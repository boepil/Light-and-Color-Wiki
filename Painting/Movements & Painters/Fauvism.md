**Scope:** Fauvism — the "wild beasts" who liberated color from description at the 1905 Salon d'Automne, its flat high-chroma style, and Matisse's philosophy of color as expression and equilibrium.

### "Wild Beasts" and the Liberation of Color

The movement earned its name at the **1905 Salon d'Automne** in Paris, where a critic, startled by the group's aggressive color, branded them **"fauves"** — French for "wild beasts". Their program: **liberate color from reality**, reacting sharply against **local color** — "the literal appearance of an object." The artist could "paint a tree blue or red to give the object an intensity and power it would not possess in nature".

### Stylistic Hallmarks

- **Pure high-chroma color** in flat areas and broad brushstrokes; **negation of modeling** in favor of two-dimensional flatness.
- **The "Green Line":** Matisse's 1905 portrait *Madame Matisse* runs a light green line down the face's center, dividing it "into a hemisphere of 'harsh yellows' and one of natural ochre".
- **Vibrational complements:** Matisse paired a **violet eyebrow** against a dominant yellow field to heighten intensity through simultaneous contrast.
- **Color-drawing fusion:** "color and drawing became inextricably linked" — blue arabesques in *Harmony in Red* define form and space while insisting on flatness.

### Matisse's Philosophy of Color

- **Expression over representation:** color should "do everything," be "suggestive of mood rather than a 'representational element'".
- **Subjective equilibrium:** compositions balance through "simple, luminous areas" — rationally calculated beforehand, with sketches and written color notes, "only to modify them later based on his subjective feeling during the process".
- **The flatness motto:** "**Plus c'est plat, plus c'est de l'art**" — "The flatter it is, the more it is art".

### Ancestry and the Shift to Cubism

- Fauvism grew out of **Van Gogh, Gauguin, and Bonnard**, continuing Gauguin's experiments with large color areas and the flat, unmodeled symbolic style of Japanese prints (cloisonnism).
- By breaking naturalism entirely, Fauvism "paved the way for the move toward abstraction and the development of Cubism"; Georges Braque moved from Fauvism to Cubism, where the "polychromatic wealth" of Fauve color was replaced by a somber palette of ochre, brown, and blue to focus on geometric form.

### Critical Reaction

The initial response was shock and ridicule — the "fauve" label born of "a perceived lack of discipline and a departure from the 'smooth application' of traditional academic art". The movement is now recognized as foundational to modernism, shifting painting's "focus from mimicry to the witnessed performance of the artist's expressive manipulation of paint".

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's history places Fauvism inside the Newtonian "emancipation of color": movements like the Fauves used "shocking" or "forbidden" or "wild" color schemes "to break out of conventional color codes and use color to say new things" — and then, "after a brief period of argumentative turmoil, these practices became the new color norms, the new color codes, and color dogma rolled on as complacently as before." His separate point for painters: the *green line* and violet-yellow pairings are not arbitrariness but disciplined use of complementary contrast on closely matched values, of a piece with his charge that the great colorists managed "color materials" rather than doctrines *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]])*.
