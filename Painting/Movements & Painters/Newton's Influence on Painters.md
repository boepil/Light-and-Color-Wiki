**Scope:** Newton's *Opticks* and its three-century influence — the circle, the spectral doctrine, complementarity, and the artist-theorists from Le Blon and Moses Harris to Chevreul, Rood, Munsell, and Turner.

### The Foundational Text

Newton's 1704 *Opticks* is "the foundational text for the modern scientific study of color," influencing artistic practice and theory "for over three centuries" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*). Its deepest art-world contribution: replacing the Aristotelian "linear ordering between light and dark" with the **circular arrangement of hues** (*Color in Art*, *Contemporary Color*).

### Newton's Specific Contributions

- **Spectral analysis (1666):** a triangular prism showed white sunlight is a heterogeneous mixture of "**differently refrangible rays**" producing the visible spectrum (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Science of Paintings*).
- **Seven-hue circle:** red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet (ROYGBIV) — the wheel **asymmetrical, with unequal spacing** matching the proportions of the spectral bands; **white at the center** symbolizing the synthesis of all hues (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Musical analogy:** seven spectral colors aligned with the seven tones of the **diatonic scale** (A–G) (*Color in Art*, *The Art of Color*).
- **Complementarity:** observing **"Newton's Rings"** in thin plates gave the first illustration of complementarity — opposite colors like blue and orange "mix to produce a neutral white-gray light" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Science of Paintings*).

### The Artist-Theorist Chain

- **J.C. Le Blon:** adopted Newton's research to build the red-yellow-blue primary system and the first **four-color printing process** (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Moses Harris (1766):** *The Natural System of Colours* — "likely the first symmetrical color circle with primaries at the heart and complementary colors placed opposite one another" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **The circle's dynasty:** the circular format "dominated color theory into the modern era, forming the basis for the systems of Chevreul, Rood, and Munsell" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Goethe's Reaction

Goethe was "scathingly critical of Newton's objective, laboratory-based methodology": white light as composed of many colors was "absurd" and "suitable only as a children's fairy tale" — color, he argued, "was a function of the eye" to be studied "through subjective perception in the natural world rather than through prisms in a dark room" (*Theory of Colours*, *Color in Art*).

### Turner and the Pre-Raphaelites

- **Turner** engaged the Newton–Goethe conflict directly; his "controversion" of the warm-tonality doctrine — cool blues/whites enlivened by "a single point of rich crimson" — and the near-abstraction of *Rain, Steam and Speed* are the painterly outcome (*Color in Art*).
- **Pre-Raphaelites:** the movement's "unprecedentedly bright palette was rooted in the 19th-century scientific research of **George Field** into the purity and stability of pigments" — "a direct continuation of the scientific interest in the spectrum that Newton initiated" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Artists' Pigments*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's analysis of the famous diagram — Newton's "diatonic division of spectral hues (1704)" — shows the wheel's spacing was not arbitrary: Newton divided the hue circle "as a diagram (black lines), and as fractional diatonic sections (green lines); the spectral hues in wavenumber spacing are aligned to match the modern perceptual scaling in which extraspectral mixtures span one fourth of the total circumference." Fitting the spectrum so "yellow" and "blue" (cyan) fall in the center of their sections, Newton's divisions "correspond reasonably well to the prismatic spectrum (which distributes light evenly by wavenumber)" — though MacEvoy flags the caveat "these divisions are not definitive, because a different spectrum spacing is produced in the spectrum from a diffraction grating." And in the emancipation-of-color narrative, Newton is the hinge: the painter's color circle descends from his 1704 plate *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/tech13.md|tech13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*.
