**Scope:** Newton's *Opticks* and its three-century influence ג€” the circle, the spectral doctrine, complementarity, and the artist-theorists from Le Blon and Moses Harris to Chevreul, Rood, Munsell, and Turner.

### The Foundational Text

Newton's 1704 *Opticks* is "the foundational text for the modern scientific study of color," influencing artistic practice and theory "for over three centuries" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*). Its deepest art-world contribution: replacing the Aristotelian "linear ordering between light and dark" with the **circular arrangement of hues** (*Color in Art*, *Contemporary Color*).

### Newton's Specific Contributions

- **Spectral analysis (1666):** a triangular prism showed white sunlight is a heterogeneous mixture of "**differently refrangible rays**" producing the visible spectrum (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Science of Paintings*).
- **Seven-hue circle:** red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet (ROYGBIV) ג€” the wheel **asymmetrical, with unequal spacing** matching the proportions of the spectral bands; **white at the center** symbolizing the synthesis of all hues (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Musical analogy:** seven spectral colors aligned with the seven tones of the **diatonic scale** (Aג€“G) (*Color in Art*, *The Art of Color*).
- **Complementarity:** observing **"Newton's Rings"** in thin plates gave the first illustration of complementarity ג€” opposite colors like blue and orange "mix to produce a neutral white-gray light" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Science of Paintings*).

### The Artist-Theorist Chain

- **J.C. Le Blon:** adopted Newton's research to build the red-yellow-blue primary system and the first **four-color printing process** (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Moses Harris (1766):** *The Natural System of Colours* ג€” "likely the first symmetrical color circle with primaries at the heart and complementary colors placed opposite one another" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **The circle's dynasty:** the circular format "dominated color theory into the modern era, forming the basis for the systems of Chevreul, Rood, and Munsell" (*Color in Art*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Goethe's Reaction

Goethe was "scathingly critical of Newton's objective, laboratory-based methodology": white light as composed of many colors was "absurd" and "suitable only as a children's fairy tale" ג€” color, he argued, "was a function of the eye" to be studied "through subjective perception in the natural world rather than through prisms in a dark room" (*Theory of Colours*, *Color in Art*).

### Turner and the Pre-Raphaelites

- **Turner** engaged the Newtonג€“Goethe conflict directly; his "controversion" of the warm-tonality doctrine ג€” cool blues/whites enlivened by "a single point of rich crimson" ג€” and the near-abstraction of *Rain, Steam and Speed* are the painterly outcome (*Color in Art*).
- **Pre-Raphaelites:** the movement's "unprecedentedly bright palette was rooted in the 19th-century scientific research of **George Field** into the purity and stability of pigments" ג€” "a direct continuation of the scientific interest in the spectrum that Newton initiated" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Artists' Pigments*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's analysis of the famous diagram ג€” Newton's "diatonic division of spectral hues (1704)" ג€” shows the wheel's spacing was not arbitrary: Newton divided the hue circle "as a diagram (black lines), and as fractional diatonic sections (green lines); the spectral hues in wavenumber spacing are aligned to match the modern perceptual scaling in which extraspectral mixtures span one fourth of the total circumference." Fitting the spectrum so "yellow" and "blue" (cyan) fall in the center of their sections, Newton's divisions "correspond reasonably well to the prismatic spectrum (which distributes light evenly by wavenumber)" ג€” though MacEvoy flags the caveat "these divisions are not definitive, because a different spectrum spacing is produced in the spectrum from a diffraction grating." And in the emancipation-of-color narrative, Newton is the hinge: the painter's color circle descends from his 1704 plate *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/tech13.md|tech13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs tracks Newton's actual legacy into the hue systems painters adopted ג€” a chain with a date gap and a reversal:

- **Newton's circle had to become the "artists' colour wheel" before it reached painters ג€” and the first step was not the British classics but an anonymous 1708 pastel manual.** "After Newton introduced the circular dimension of hue in his *Opticks* of 1704, it was only a small step to arrange the three hues of the seventeenth century artists' linear scale around a circle." That step was first taken "in an anonymous chapter on pastel painting added to the 1708 Hague edition of the anonymous *Traite de la Peinture en Mignature*," which contains a pair of hand-coloured circles with seven and twelve equal divisions. The author hesitated over whether there were really three primitive colours or four (yellow, blue and two reds ג€” "fire red" or vermilion, and crimson); in the twelve-hue circle the mixed "primary" red and blue were placed evenly spaced around the circle. This twelve-hue figure is "the earliest example of the so-called 'artists' colour wheel'" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*.
- **The painters' circle reversed Newton's order ג€” and rejected his rules.** In both the 1708 diagrams "the clockwise sequence of hues follows the order of the seventeenth century linear scale (yellow-red-blue), and so is reversed compared to Newton's spectral order (red-yellow-blue)." More importantly, "while it incorporated Newton's discovery that hues form a closed loop, the 'artists' colour wheel' was otherwise an implicit rejection of the assumption that Newton's circle and rules of additive mixing (in which all spectral hues are 'primary') also applied to paints" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*.
- **Complementarity came later, and as a conflation.** The 1708 text attached no significance to opposing colours beyond not mixing distant hues; only with Harris (c. 1770ג€“72) "were claimed to reveal colours of maximum visual contrast, colours of afterimages, *and* colourant-mixing complements" simultaneously. In reality the late-nineteenth-century revolution showed the opponent, additive and colourant-mixing "opposites" are distinct sets of hues requiring "somewhat different" hue circles ג€” "this assumption of the 'all-purpose' nature of complementary pairs remains typical of simplistic traditional colour theory today" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*.
- **Traditional theory had to *counter* Newton to keep RYB.** Traditional colour theory "readily adopted Newton's hue circle of 1704 in the form of the artists' 'colour wheel'"; but Newton's finding that green and orange lights could be physically simple as well as compound was answered by asserting the spectrum is really made of separate red, yellow and blue rays whose intermixture produces the rest (the RYB-ray model that also mothered the first trichromatic receptor theory). "This hypothesis was not overturned until 1852," when Helmholtz demolished Brewster's version *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/112.md|112.html]])*.
- **Itten froze the pre-Newtonian compromise.** In *The Art of Color* (1961) Itten "reverted to a system comprising evenly-spaced historical primaries and secondaries, with six intermediates" ג€” a post-Bauhaus regression from his own 1921 colour star (which followed Hoelzel's science-derived 12-hue system). Briggs is blunt: the book "embodies an approach to colour theory and classification fixated at a stage at least seventy years before the founding of the Bauhaus," its primaries are "so remote from ideal cyan and (especially) magenta that paints of these hues make a poor set of colourant-mixing primaries," and "in several ways it is the colour theory equivalent of so-called 'creation science.'" The RYB primaries persist only as "an important historical stepping stone" toward the four psychological primaries *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/112.md|112.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag:** the page's *A Comprehensive Overviewג€¦* claim that Moses Harris's 1766 *The Natural System of Colours* is "likely the first symmetrical color circle" clashes with Briggs's documentary evidence: the earliest regularly-spaced artists' colour wheel (twelve equal divisions around the historical primaries) is the anonymous 1708 Hague *Traitֳ© de la Peinture en Mignature* plate. Harris's c. 1770ג€“72 circles are early, but the 1708 twelve-hue wheel ג€” and its placement of primaries at even intervals ג€” precedes them by half a century.
