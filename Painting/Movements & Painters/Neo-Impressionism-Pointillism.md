**Scope:** Neo-Impressionism and Pointillism — Seurat's divisionist program to "reconstitute light" with pure dots, its Chevreul–Rood foundations, and its critics.

### Divisionism and the Pointillé Technique

- **Divisionism** separates color "into its individual spectral components rather than blending them into a single tone on a palette" — tiny dots of pure color (**pointillé**) applied so that colors "merge in the viewer's eye rather than on the canvas" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Color in Art*).
- The merged result creates "a **flicker or glimmer** known as **lustre**" — "a soft brilliancy and a certain appearance of transparency" (*Color in Art*).
- **Dot spacing is critical:** dots must be small enough and placed close enough to "literally touch each other"; with space between them the eye reads separate colors instead of mixing (*The Art of Color*).
- **Chromatic grays:** instead of gray pigment, juxtaposed complementary dots (e.g., red and green) blend at a distance "into a vibrant, harmonious gray" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Seurat's Program: Reconstituting Light

Seurat called himself an **"impressioniste-luministe"** — his goal the **"reconstitution of light"** through color (*Color in Art*, *The Science of Paintings*). His quasi-scientific method stood on two foundations:

- **Chevreul's Law of Simultaneous Contrast (1839):** the eye "spontaneously generates the complement of any given color," so adjacent colors mutually shift perceived hue and intensity (*The Art of Color*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Rood's spinning discs:** Ogden Rood's rotating Maxwell discs proved pigments could be combined visually through persistence of vision — "**spatial additive mixing**" with "far greater luminosity than traditional subtractive palette mixing" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Science of Paintings*).
- Seurat extended the system even to the **picture frames**, dotting their borders "to enhance the contrast and luminosity of the canvas masses" (*Color in Art*).

### Critiques and the Rejection of Pointillism

- **Static and mechanical:** critics ridiculed the "machine-like regularity" and "painstakingly systematic brushwork" with a "stiff formality" that seemed to ape mass production (*Modern Art*, *Color in Art*).
- **Loss of vibration:** the effect was "very quiet" — "congealed," sacrificing the Impressionists' visceral vibration (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Rood's dismay:** the physicist himself was "appalled" at the application of his theories, telling his son he wished he had never written his book (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **The gray-sum:** because the "sum of all the spectral dots often approaches a middle gray rather than pure white light," there is "a perceived loss of color intensity in the final work" (*A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- Cézanne rejected the dot as "too static," returning to continuous color areas integrated "more rhythmically" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., The Art of Color*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's mixing rules give the pointillists' ambition and its failure in one breath: rule 39 — "two paint colors on opposite sides of the hue circle will mix an achromatic or near neutral color" — and rule 40 — "the mixture of two paints will always be duller than the more saturated paint and darker than the lighter valued paint." Juxtaposed complements therefore cannot stay "pure": at viewing distance they average to the very gray the Neo-Impressionists claimed to avoid, exactly the "gray-sum" critique. MacEvoy's historical framing: the European tradition called such desaturating mixtures **broken colors** — "breaking a color was synonymous with dulling or whitening it" — so the pointillist "chromatic gray" is really an optically re-invented broken color. And the perceptual scaling point stands behind the "vibration" loss: luminous frequency spans less than one octave (400–750 THz) compared to sound's ~10 octaves — color's "music" cannot vibrate like music *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/tech13.md|tech13.html]])*.
