**Scope:** The painter's toolkit of color composition — harmony, balance, dominance, proportion frameworks, contrast types, unifying neutrals, temperature structure, and the critique of geometric harmony schemes.

### Harmony, Balance, Dominance, Key

- **Harmony** is often defined as an "analogy of opposites" — "a state of equilibrium where colors are in inherent agreement" (*A Comprehensive Overview of Color Vision Mechanisms, Color Spaces, and the Munsell System*, *Contemporary Color*).
- **Balance** is achieved either by centering color points at a **neutral balancing point** (the center of the color solid) or through **compensation of quantity**: "small areas of high-intensity color balance large fields of weak chroma" (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Dominance** is established by "the largest color or value group in a composition"; a painting's **key** follows — "a high-key image has a higher proportion of light values, while a low-key image is dominated by darks" (*Contemporary Color*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Proportion Frameworks

- The **"mostly, some and a bit"** formula: use one dominant hue or temperature, a secondary amount of another, and "a small accent of a third contrasting color to create interest without confusion" (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Itten's area proportions** derive from luminosity — **yellow:red:blue = 3:6:8** (*The Art of Color*).
- **Munsell** treated area as an element of balance: pigment inequalities of hue and chroma "require different extensions to reach a neutral gray mixture" (*A Color Notation*).
- **Limited palette** is recommended as discipline: "restricted means" often grant the artist more freedom (*Contemporary Color*, *The Science of Paintings*).

### The Seven Contrasts (Itten) and Chevreul's Law

Johannes Itten codified seven distinct types of contrast (*The Art of Color*): **contrast of hue** (undiluted colors at peak intensity), **light–dark** (polarity of black, white, gray), **cold–warm** (temperature polarities), **complementary** ("opposites that incite each other to maximum vividness"), **simultaneous** (the eye generating a complement in adjacent areas), **saturation** (intense vs. dull), and **extension** (relative areas of color patches). Michel-Eugène Chevreul's earlier contribution — the **Law of Simultaneous Contrast** (1839) — established that colors "influence each other and their after-images predictably when placed side-by-side," a finding that became "the foundation for Neo-Impressionist optical mixing" (*Color in Art*, see [[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing]]).

### Neutrals, Grays, Earths — and Mother Color

- Neutral gray acts as a **"sterile neuter"** that "mellows violent oppositions," taking on character only from its neighbors through simultaneous contrast (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Mother color** — "a single color or pigment added to every other color in a palette" — influences the entire scene and connects disparate shapes; it mimics natural light, "where richer effects are enveloped in an atmosphere of gray" or "lilac grays" (*Plein Air Techniques for Artists*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).

### Temperature as Structure

- **Warm colors advance** — "appearing larger and closer"; **cool colors recede** (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Atmospheric perspective:** in landscapes, colors become cooler as they recede from the eye (*Contemporary Color*).
- **Focal areas:** high-contrast temperature shifts — a cool color next to a warm one — at the center of interest "naturally draw the eye and act as a 'magnet' for attention" (*Contemporary Color*).

### The Critique of Geometric Harmony

The geometric schemes (complementary pairs, triads, tetrads inscribed in the 12-hue circle) are widely taught but heavily criticized:

- **Itten** defended them as "objective principles of harmony" (*The Art of Color*).
- **Albers** dismissed these "rules of thumb" as **"worn out"** and mechanically rigid: any color can "go" with any other if quantities are right (*Interaction of Color*).
- **Munsell** rejected the Brewster (red-yellow-blue) theory as **"unbalanced" and "distorted"** — it ignores the real inequalities of pigment chroma (*A Color Notation*).
- **Gage:** complementary contrast was a "catechism" of harmony, "but its scientific basis is not entirely convincing" — and great colorists like **Turner** "controverted the old doctrine of the balance of colors" with deliberately unbalanced, predominantly cool tonalities (*Color in Art*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy frames the same critique from inside the palette: harmony formulas drawn from geometry fail because the color solid itself is irregular — chroma capabilities differ by hue, lightness binds chroma unevenly, and complements are not visual equals (afterimages are paler than their stimuli, so the complement relationship "is not symmetrical"). His working alternative to formulaic harmony is quantity control in a real color space: "colors that provide the largest contrast are widely separated in lightness and chromaticity," and the durable advice is to build compositions from strong value structure plus carefully rationed chroma rather than wheel geometry *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/tech13.md|tech13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*.
