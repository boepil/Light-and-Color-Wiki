**Scope:** The physical stage of painting — supports (paper, canvas, panels) and grounds (sizing, gesso, primers) organized by medium: watercolor, oil, and other media, and how the support's tone, absorbency, and texture determine color appearance.

### Watercolor: paper is the palette
Watercolor lives and dies by its paper. The sheet is simultaneously the support and the "white" of the palette — water itself dilutes every mixture toward the paper's white, so the paper's lightness, tint, and reflectivity set the highest value and the gamut of everything painted on it (*David Bellamy's Watercolour Landscape Course*, *Mastering Atmosphere & Mood in Watercolor*). The largest gamut comes from a bright, untinted, well-sized paper that holds paint on the surface instead of soaking it into the fibers; a gray or absorbent paper visibly shrinks the range of achievable colors (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).

- **Weight:** quoted in **pounds per ream (lb)** or **grams per square meter (gsm)**. 72 lb is thin, 90 lb intermediate, **140 lb is the practical minimum for all-around work**; **250–400 lb board-weight sheets** behave like boards and need no stretching. Handprint's range note: typical watercolor papers run **300–600 gsm**, and above 600 gsm is board weight; fragile papers warp more when repeatedly wetted and are more absorptive (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).
- **Finish** — three standard surfaces: **Hot Pressed (HP)** — smooth, sealed by heated rollers, less absorbent, best for fine detail and subtle granulation, but encourages backruns/water marks in wet paint; **Cold Pressed (CP)** — the all-around standard with moderate texture; **Rough (R)** — heavily textured, accents brush texture and coarse granulation. Finishes vary wildly between brands — "look at the paper surface, not at the label" (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).
- **Fiber:** the highest quality papers are **100% cotton or linen**; wood-pulp and mixed papers are cheaper and less durable. Infallible test: pure cotton/linen paper burns to a **white, wispy ash**; a black brittle ash indicates wood pulp or synthetics. **Lignin** (a wood-fiber component) is the major cause of yellowing newsprint — "lignin free" papers have had nearly all of it removed (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).
- **Sizing:** a coating that slows the absorption ("creep") of water beyond the brush mark. **Internal sizing** is added to the pulp before casting; **surface (tub) sizing** is applied after drying; **unsized papers** (blotters) absorb paint immediately. Staining behavior is heavily influenced by the amount and type of sizing (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).
- **Acidity & permanence:** acidic papers degrade — neutral to slightly alkaline (pH 7.5–10) is desirable; an "alkaline reserve" (calcium carbonate) stabilizes pH after manufacture. Unlike oils, watercolors **cannot be relined**, so paper permanence is primary (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).
- **Formats:** **blocks** are pre-gummed on all sides and act as an "already stretched" surface (*David Bellamy's Watercolour Landscape Course*); sheets are stretched by soaking and stapling; heavy rag boards (250–400 lb) take severe treatment with no stretching (*Plein Air Techniques for Artists*).

### Oil: canvas, ground, and the size beneath
Oil painting is a layered system — **support → size → ground → paint** — where each layer must be chemically and mechanically compatible (*Ralph Mayer — The Artist's Handbook*).

- **Canvas:** **linen** is the professional standard — strong, square-woven, distinct texture; **cotton duck** is inferior, stretches poorly and feels flimsy (*Ralph Mayer*). Rigid panels were the Northern European tradition (oak) and Italian tradition (poplar); modern **Presdwood (Masonite)** is homogeneous, resists warping, and does not split (*Carlson's Guide to Landscape Painting*).
- **Sizing:** raw fabric must first be sealed with **animal glue size (hide or rabbit-skin glue)** so the oil binder never touches the fibers directly — contact causes the canvas to rot and become brittle (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Ground:** seals the sized support, blocks binder absorption, and supplies the **tooth** (granular roughness) that grips brushstrokes. Traditional **oil ground: white lead thinned with turpentine** — semi-matte and slightly absorbent. **Gesso** (glue + chalk) is believed by some to give superior luminosity, but it lacks flexibility on canvas and can crackle or flake (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Stretching:** canvases are tensioned with **wooden keys (wedges)** driven into the corners of the stretcher; never key up in a cold room, where brittle sizing and paint can crack (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Unusual supports:** metals (copper, aluminum) were used for small, detailed oils, though adhesion is precarious (*The Science of Paintings*).

### Other media: grounds engineered to the binder
- **Acrylic:** adheres to nearly any grease-free surface. The flexible polymer film tolerates the expansion of stretched canvas; most modern "gesso" is actually an **acrylic polymer emulsion primer** — non-absorbent, unlike traditional glue gesso — usable on panels, paper, or raw linen (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Pastel:** needs a ground with serious **tooth** to file off and hold dry pigment — fibrous papers, granular papers coated with fine inert grit, or flocked (velvety) papers; **tinted papers** supply a mid-tone for the drawing (*Carlson's Guide*).
- **Gouache:** paper or rigid panels; the opaque, matte, brittle film means it depends less on ground reflectivity than transparent watercolor (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt7.md|pigmt7.html]])*).
- **Egg tempera:** because the film has no flexibility, it demands **rigid panels with absorbent glue gesso** or it cracks and flakes (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Ink & scratchboard:** stiff cardboard coated with a **clay or whiting gesso** that takes India ink and can be carved back (*Chinese Ink Painting: Techniques*).
- **Non-traditional:** **Mylar** appears as a contemporary restoration facing; copper and aluminum panels for small oils (*The Science of Paintings*).

### How the ground shapes the color
A **brilliant white ground is an "internal light"** — photons pass through translucent paint, strike the white, and return, creating luminosity; this is the physics behind watercolor's stained-glass look (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*). Conversely an **imprimatura** — a thin veil of color over the ground — kills the "disturbing" white, establishing the value and color key of the whole composition (*Carlson's Guide*, *Imaginative Realism*). The ground's absorbency also sets how much binder is pulled out of each brushstroke, changing chroma and drying shift (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).

## Handprint Perspectives
MacEvoy treats the support as one half of the watercolor equation: the paint's attributes — transparency, staining, granulation — are **only meaningful relative to the paper they are applied to**. Staining, for example, is redefined by the sheet: a paint that is staining on a rough, lightly sized paper can be non-staining on a smooth, well-sized one, because the same pigment either sits on the surface or is dragged deep between the fibers (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*). He insists that the finish label is unreliable — CP of one brand may behave like Rough of another — and that bright-white papers should be avoided because the fluorescent brighteners are impermanent dyes. For his own lightfastness testing he chose Arches block watercolor paper on fir plywood under UV-transmitting acrylic, treating the paper as a controlled variable (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/litetest.md|litetest.html]])*). *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag:** The notebook corpus praises gesso for luminosity (*Ralph Mayer — The Artist's Handbook*), while the same source warns gesso lacks flexibility and crackles on canvas. The resolution is material-specific: glue gesso belongs on rigid panels (and is required for tempera), while flexible acrylic primer belongs on canvas — a distinction Mayer makes explicit.
