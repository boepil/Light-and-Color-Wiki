**Scope:** The artist's delivery system — brush anatomy, hair types, and shapes organized by medium: watercolor, oil, and other media, with sizes and care practices.

### Anatomy: hair, ferrule, handle
A brush has three parts — the **hair**, the **ferrule**, and the **handle** — and the hair determines the mark-making capability (*Ralph Mayer — The Artist's Handbook*). High-quality brushes set the hair in **vulcanized rubber** within a **brass or copper ferrule** crimped to a wooden handle; watercolor handles are short for close work, oil handles long for standing at an easel (*Ralph Mayer*).

- **Kolinsky red sable** — from the tail of the Siberian mink; the finest hair for its resilience, "snap," and sharp point. Quality hair is expensive: about **$1,500 per pound** before manufacture (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Squirrel** — very soft, less snap; excellent for holding liquid media like inks or dyes (*Chinese Ink Painting*).
- **Hog bristle** — stiff, with split ends ("flags") that hold thick paint; the traditional oil-painting hair (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Nylon/synthetic** — increasingly sophisticated; the standard for acrylics because the polymer emulsion is destructive to natural hair (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Ox hair** — from cattle ears; stiffer and springier than sable; large, inexpensive brushes (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **"Camel hair"** — a trade name for blends of soft hairs (squirrel, horse, goat); real camel hair is too coarse to paint with (*Ralph Mayer*).

### Shapes
- **Round** — circular ferrule, pointed tip (watercolor) or blunt (oil); lines, details, small shapes (*Ralph Mayer*, *Betty Edwards*).
- **Flat** — flattened ferrule, straight edge; length about **twice the width** (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Bright** — short-haired flat with sharp corners; length about **1.5× width**; ideal for "pushing about" thick, buttery paint (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Filbert** — flat with rounded corners; soft edges and blending (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Fan** — sparsely bristled, fan-shaped; used clean and dry to soften contours or blend wet paint (*Carlson's Guide*).
- **Liner/rigger** — very long-haired thin round; twigs, outlines, signing, long fluid lines (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Mop** — large, soft (squirrel/goat); holds copious water for broad washes (*David Bellamy's Watercolour Landscape Course*).
- **Badger blender** — flared brush used perpendicular to the surface for imperceptible gradations and thinning glazes (*Ralph Mayer*).

### Watercolor: soft hair, big water reservoirs
Watercolor brushes must be **soft and hold a large water reservoir**; even large brushes need a **pointed tip** for control (*David Bellamy*, *Mastering Atmosphere & Mood in Watercolor*). Use a brush "almost too big" for the shape — e.g. a **1-inch flat** — to build a **bead** of paint that flows down the paper, preserving luminosity and avoiding "dead" color (*Mastering Atmosphere & Mood in Watercolor*). **Dampness control** is the core skill: squeezing half the charge out of the brush between the fingers gives uniform translucent strokes and prevents drips (*David Bellamy*). A **damp brush or sponge lifts** wet pigment to reveal white paper for highlights (*David Bellamy*, *Plein Air Techniques*). Handprint's test methods rely on standard tools: a **½" flat brush** for primary-paint squares, a **1" flat** for washes and water measuring, and a **small (#6) damp sable** for cleaning pan surfaces (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*, (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt5.md|pigmt5.html]])*).

### Oil: stiff scrub-in, sable glazes
Oil technique splits the brushwork: **stiff bristle** brushes scrub in the initial thick paint; **sable** brushes handle glazes, precise details, and smooth flat strokes that won't lift the underpainting (*Carlson's Guide*, *Ralph Mayer*). Between color changes artists rinse in **kerosene or mineral spirits** and wipe excess with **muslin rags** (*Ralph Mayer*). The "bright" shape exists specifically for pushing thick, buttery paint (*Ralph Mayer*).

### Other media: synthetics for acrylic, fine sable for tempera
- **Acrylic:** **synthetic nylon** is standard — the acrylic polymer emulsion destroys natural hair (*Ralph Mayer*). Because acrylic dries into an insoluble film, brushes must be **kept submerged in water while painting** to keep polymer out of the ferrule (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Gouache:** thicker than transparent watercolor — a **stiffer synthetic or acrylic brush** may work better than natural hair, and the same brush should not serve both gouache and watercolor when the gouache has white filler (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt7.md|pigmt7.html]])*).
- **Egg tempera:** requires the **finest red sable** watercolor brushes; the classic technique hatches with the point (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Pastel:** stiff bristle brushes, sometimes trimmed short, spread and blend dry pigment (*Carlson's Guide*).
- **Lifting & corrections:** watercolor corrections are made by wetting and gently scrubbing with a **stiff synthetic-bristle flat brush**, then blotting with an undyed paper towel — effective only with non-staining paints (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*).

### Sizes, numbers, and care
Brush sizes are numbered (**No. 0 to No. 30**; larger number = larger brush); some flats are sized by width in fractions of an inch (*Ralph Mayer*). Care: rinse thoroughly in the appropriate solvent or running water immediately after use; clean with mild soap (**Murphy's Oil Soap** or yellow household soap) until the water runs clear; **never leave a brush resting on its hair** in a container — the bristles bend permanently; store upright in a glass or flat in a box; use **mothballs (naphthalene)** when storing natural-hair brushes long-term (*Ralph Mayer*). Never point a brush with the lips — toxic pigments can be ingested that way (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]])*).

## Handprint Perspectives
MacEvoy's brush advice is procedural, not poetic: brushes are measuring instruments and correction tools as much as paint carriers. His pigment-testing method leans on the **1" flat acrylic brush** as a calibrated water measure ("roughly the amount carried by a fully charged 1" flat"), on a **½" flat** for consistency in swatch work, and on a **1/8" flat** for precise daubing — and he insists on **thorough rinsing between colors** and watching how quickly paint rinses from the brush as a quality signal (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt3.md|pigmt3.html]])*, (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt9.md|pigmt9.html]])*). His lifting recipe is equally specific: wet, scrub with a stiff synthetic flat, blot with undyed paper towel — only for non-staining paints (*(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*). *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt3.md|pigmt3.html]])*

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag:** The notebook corpus (via *Ralph Mayer*) names hog bristle as the standard oil hair, while *Carlson's Guide* also endorses sable for smooth oil work — not a contradiction, but a warning against assuming a single oil brush; the sources agree bristle scrubs, sable glazes.
