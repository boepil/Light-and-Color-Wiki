**Scope:** The intertwined history of pigments, the science of light and colour, and painting practice — from the earth pigments of prehistoric walls, through the small palettes of antiquity and Vermeer's oil-painting luminosity, to 19th-century synthetic chemistry and the Impressionists, the trickle-down of colorimetry into the studio, and the standardized present.

### Prehistory & Antiquity — the elemental palette

The earliest art was made with **charcoal and iron-oxide ochre** scratched onto cave walls, before hand-finished pigment production existed (*Contemporary Color: Theory and Applications*, Fig. 5-1). At **Lascaux (France, c. 15,000 BC)**, **Altamira (Spain)** and the **Cougnac cave (c. 20,000 years ago)** the palette was confined to reds, yellows and earth tones from clay, minerals and burnt animal bone, bound with animal fat, saliva, or the natural stickiness of clay-bearing earths (*Color for Science, Art, and Technology*, Ch. 6 "Color in Folklore and Anthropology" — ochre already used by *Homo erectus* some 300,000 years ago). Antiquity kept a "small palette" built from:

- **Minerals:** malachite (green), azurite (blue), cinnabar (native vermilion), orpiment (arsenic yellow) and the prized lapis lazuli (natural ultramarine) — ground to powder, later processed by the wax-kneading method of Cennini (*The Artist's Handbook of Materials and Techniques*, §2 "Pigments"; *Artists' Pigments: A Handbook of Their History and Characteristics*, monographs on "Indian Yellow", "Red Lead and Minium", "Green Earth").
- **Organics:** madder lake (root red), indigo (plant blue), kermes and cochineal (insect carmine), and **sepia from squid ink**; **Tyrian purple** from *Murex* mollusks was the most valued dyestuff of the ancient world for its costly extraction and imperial association (*Artists' Pigments*).
- **Media:** **egg tempera** became the standard for panel painting and **encaustic** (wax liquefied by heat) produced the Fayum mummy portraits of Graeco-Roman Egypt (c. 2nd century BC – 2nd century AD) (*The Science of Paintings*, §2.3 "Tempera", §2.4 "Encaustic"; Mayer, §5 and §7).

### The Age of Oil & Vermeer — a new luminosity

Oil painting was **not van Eyck's invention**: it developed gradually from at least the **13th century**, as in the Norwegian altar frontals painted with a drying-oil/egg mixture; by the 15th century Rogier van der Weyden (c. 1435) used **linseed oil** for unprecedented depth and realistic texture, and later research disproved the 1781 "van Eyck invents oil" legend (*The Science of Paintings*, §3.3 "Oils"; Mayer, §1 "Introductory Notes"). **Johannes Vermeer (1632–1675)** was a pinnacle of this technical evolution: scrupulous about materials, he repeatedly used the expensive natural ultramarine alongside yellow ochre (*Color in Art* — John Gage, Ch. 1), and his work shows an advanced mastery of light "edges" and diffraction — Carlson calls him the summit of understanding edges or halation in paint (*Carlson's Guide to Landscape Painting*, "The Palette"). Tellingly, Vermeer paired blue and yellow at a time when his compatriot Christiaan Huygens theorized these as the two fundamental constituents of light (*Color in Art*). See [[Vermeer-Dutch Golden Age]].

### Scientific Revolution & the 19th-century synthetic palette

- **New theories of light:** Newton's *Opticks* (1704) presented the first color circle, born from the 1666 prism demonstrations that white light is a mixture of seven spectral hues (*Color in Art*; handprint treats this circle as "the foundation image for artists' color wheels and the hue circle in all scientific color spaces"). Goethe's *Theory of Colours* (1810) reacted against Newton, insisting on the subjective, physiological experience of color, including colored shadows (*Contemporary Color*, Ch. 2).
- **The chemistry boom:** **Prussian blue (c. 1704)** was the first documented synthetic pigment; it was followed by **cobalt blue (1802)**, synthetic **ultramarine (1828)**, **viridian (1838)**, and **cerulean blue (1860)**; the yellows and reds were transformed by **chromium yellows (c. 1803)**, **cadmium yellow (discovered 1817, commercial 1840s)**, **alizarin crimson (1868)** and **cadmium red (c. 1910)**; **Perkin's mauve (1856)** was the first commercial colorant made from coal tar (*Mayer*, §2 "Pigments"; *The Science of Paintings*, Tables 8.2–8.3). See [[Natural vs. Synthetic]], [[PY35 - Cadmium Yellow]], [[PR83 - Alizarin Crimson]], [[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue]].
- **Impressionism & plein air:** James Rand's **collapsible paint tube (1841 patent)** freed Claude Monet and the Impressionists from the studio, enabling the **plein air** movement and the separation of the artist from manufacturing (*Contemporary Color*, Ch. 5; *The Science of Paintings*, §2.1). The bright new synthetics let painters render shifting atmospheric light and literal **colored shadows** (blue shadows on snow), replacing the muted studio palette. See [[Impressionism]] and [[Neo-Impressionism-Pointillism]].

### The trickle-down of color science

Scientific results reached the studio and the academy slowly but inexorably:

- **Michel Eugène Chevreul (1786–1889),** chemist and director of dyeing at the Gobelins tapestry works, published the **Law of Simultaneous Contrast** (1839) — a "catechism of complementarity" that directly fed Vincent van Gogh and Georges Seurat (*Color in Art*, Ch. 1; *Contemporary Color*, Ch. 2).
- **James Clerk Maxwell** advanced the **trichromatic** theory of color vision; **Ogden Rood**'s *Modern Chromatics* (1879) argued colors can be mixed **optically in the eye** through persistence of vision — the principle behind Pointillism (*Color in Art*, Ch. 1; *Ordering Colour: Albert Henry Munsell*).
- **Albert H. Munsell (1858–1918),** American painter and teacher, replaced the wheel with the **color tree**, specifying color by **Hue, Value, and Chroma** on a scale of equal visual spacing — in *A Color Notation* (1905), later fixed numerically by the 1943 Munsell Renotation (*A Comprehensive Overview of Color Vision Mechanisms, Color Spaces, and the Munsell System*). See [[Munsell Notation]].
- **Standardization:** the **CIE 1931 standard observer** gave a mathematical frame for describing color, and **colorimetry** matured through the theoretical **optimal colors** of Erwin Schrödinger, the just-noticeable-difference **ellipses** and **MacAdam limits** of David MacAdam, and Michael Pointer's empirical survey of real surface colors (1980) — a genealogy detailed in *Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits* and *Color for Science, Art, and Technology*. See [[CIE Systems]], [[MacAdam Ellipses]], [[Colors/Gamuts/Optimal Color Solid-MacAdam Limits|Optimal Color Solid-MacAdam Limits]], [[Pointer's Gamut]].

### The present — modern synthetics & archival standards

Today the palette is dominated by **synthetic organic pigments** unmatched in intensity and permanence: the **phthalocyanines** (blue 1935, green 1938), the **quinacridones** (e.g. "Acra" red shades) and the **pyrroles** (*Mayer*, Ch. 12 "The New Materials"; *Color for Science, Art, and Technology*, Color Plate 28). Paint tubes are now legible instruments: **Colour Index (CI) generic names** such as PB28 (cobalt blue), constitution numbers, **lightfastness ratings** and overdose label conformances turn the palette into a documented archive (*Artists' Pigments: A Handbook*, "Standard Specifications of Pigment Composition and Color"). Archival practice rests on technical examination — X-ray and infrared imaging that reads pigments, ground and condition without touching the paint film (*Mayer*, §2). See [[Transparency, Opacity & Pigment Codes]], [[PR122 - Quinacridone Magenta]], [[PR254 - Pyrrole Red]].

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy frames the last three centuries as "truly amazing advances in pigment chemistry" — his recommended history being Philip Ball's *Bright Earth: The Art and Invention of Color* *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*. He stresses that **primary pigments are a moving target set by manufacturing**: Le Blon's early-1700s subtractive printing system used a carmine/madder-lake/vermilion red plus yellow lake and iron blue (PB27); Moses Harris's wheel of 1772 used vermilion, orpiment and natural ultramarine; Johann Lambert in 1785 preferred gamboge, carmine and iron blue; Charles Winter's mixing triangle used alizarin crimson, cadmium lemon and phthalo blue; a contemporary painter would choose benzimidazolone yellow, quinacridone magenta and phthalo cyan. The "true primary red" thus marched from vermilion (PR106) and madder (NR4), to alizarin crimson (PR83) in the late 19th century, to the modern recommendation of quinacridone (PR122/PV19) — all of them far from the ideal magenta hue, and the "primary blue" has only traveled about halfway toward ideal cyan *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*. MacEvoy also flags Newton's 1704 circle as the ancestor of every artists' wheel and scientific hue circle, but one that entangled three unresolved problems — the diatonic musical spacing, the additive "measurable light mixtures" it was invented to explain, and a self-defeating notion of primaries that made material and radiant color appear identical; painters who stuffed RYB into the circle made additive and subtractive mixing look like the same thing. On the chemistry itself, he notes the synthetic organics were a major second-half-of-the-19th-century innovation but many of the first coal-tar colorants proved too impermanent for art, while the modern 20th-century synthetics are far more durable — "with very few exceptions, all commercial artists' paints use synthetic pigments" today, and the Society of Dyers and Colourists publishes the standard Colour Index names *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt1.md|pigmt1.html]])*.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs compresses the intellectual arc into the **"late 19th century Helmholtz–Maxwell–Hering revolution"**: modern colour theory was settled then, and today's revival of traditional colour theory is "not based on a successful challenge to the truth of modern colour theory" but on art education's concurrent retreat from anatomy, perspective and drawing — its relationship to modern theory being "in some ways like that of so-called 'Creation Science' to modern biology": not science-versus-art, but "the sciences of different eras" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]])*. Tracing the primaries themselves, Briggs roots the painter's "mix everything from three" premise in **Aristotle's *Meteorologica* (c. 350 BC)** — whose three were red, green and blue-violet — with red/yellow/blue established among painters and dyers by the first decades of the 17th century and the first hue circle of artists' paints appearing in the anonymous 1708 Hague pastel manual; **Newton proved the "intermixture model" untenable** as physics (every hue except purple can be either simple or compound light), but left the puzzle of *why three* colourants are optimal, a gulf filled until **Helmholtz (1852, 1866)** and **Young** located the answer in three overlapping cone sensitivities rather than in three "rays" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/062.md|062.html]])*.
