**Scope:** One history with four engines — art practice, pigment production, the science of light, and culture — and how the era that led each one pushed color from trade good to measured system.

### What this page is about

Color did not get its modern systems in a laboratory first and reach painters later. For most of history it worked the other way around: **people made colors because they needed them** — for ritual, status, trade, and picture-making — and only much later did anyone try to measure light and color with numbers.

This page tells that story as **one loop with four engines** running at once:

- **Art practice** — what painters and cultures wanted to show (see [[Painting/index|Painting]])
- **Pigment production** — what craftspeople and factories could manufacture (see [[Pigments/index|Pigments]])
- **Science of light** — how physicists and physiologists explained what was happening (see [[Light/index|Light]] and [[Eye/index|Eye]])
- **Culture** — economics, status, and meaning, why lapis and Tyrian purple mattered (see [[Intersections/index|Intersections]] and [[Color Psychology & Symbolism]])

Different eras were led by different engines. Prehistory was culture and trade; the oil age was refined studio practice; the 19th century was chemistry; the 20th brought factory-born colorimetry back into the studio; today preservation and forensic science close the loop.

The sections below go deep — names, dates, citations, and links to movement and pigment pages. **You do not need to read it all at once.** Use the chapter guide to jump to an era.

### The story in five chapters

Read straight through, or scan for the era you care about:

1. **Prehistory & Antiquity** — ochre caves, the small mineral and organic palette, Tyrian purple and lapis economics (color before theory)
2. **The Age of Oil & Vermeer** — oil painting matures; Vermeer's materials meet Huygens's light theory in the same town
3. **Scientific Revolution & the 19th-century synthetic boom** — Newton and Goethe; **[[PB27 - Prussian Blue|Prussian blue]]** to alizarin; tubes and plein-air Impressionism
4. **The trickle-down of colorimetry** — Chevreul, Maxwell, Rood, Munsell, CIE — industry-born science returns to art
5. **The present** — phthalos, quinacridones, CI codes on tubes, X-ray and IR forensics

the history of color is not three parallel histories — it is **one loop with four engines**: art practice (demand), pigment production (craft becoming industry), the science of light (theory), and culture (meaning, status, economics). The major point of the story is that each era was led by a different engine: ritual and trade before any theory; practice outrunning theory in the oil age; chemistry as the hinge of the 19th century; industry-born colorimetry standardizing the studio in the 20th; and archival science closing the loop today. **The corpus states the thesis outright**: color is a "major connecting bridge" between science, technology and the arts, works of art are the "most fertile ground" for understanding color's interdisciplinary dimensions, and scientific analysis lets us look "beyond the eye" to the genesis of an artist's choices.

### Prehistory & Antiquity — culture and trade before theory

- pigment was the first technology of color, and it belonged to **culture and craft** — the palette was set by ritual, status and economics centuries before anyone theorized about light.
- The earliest art was made with **charcoal and iron-oxide ochre** scratched onto cave walls, before hand-finished pigment production existed. **Ochre is older than art**: *Homo erectus* was collecting it some **300,000 years ago** in France, and Neanderthals used it in burials roughly **70,000 years ago**. In the Upper Palaeolithic (c. 10,000–30,000 BP) the artists of **Lascaux (France, c. 15,000 BC)**, **Altamira (Spain)** and the **Cougnac cave (c. 20,000 years ago)** worked a palette of reds, yellows and browns from ochres, black from charcoal and white from marl, bound with animal fat, saliva, or the natural stickiness of clay-bearing earths. See [[PY43 - Yellow Ochre]], [[PR101 - Mars Red]], [[PBk6 - Carbon Black]].
- Antiquity kept a "small palette" built from:
  - **Minerals:** malachite (green), azurite (blue), cinnabar (native vermilion), orpiment (arsenic yellow) and the prized lapis lazuli (natural ultramarine), used in Europe by the **12th century** — ground to powder, later processed by the wax-kneading method of Cennini. See [[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue]].
  - **Organics:** madder lake (root red), indigo (plant blue), kermes and cochineal (insect carmine), and **sepia from squid ink**; **Tyrian purple** from *Murex* mollusks was the most valued dyestuff of the ancient world for its costly extraction and imperial association.
  - **Media:** **egg tempera** became the standard for panel painting, carried by Italian masters from **Giotto (14th century)** onward, and **encaustic** (wax liquefied by heat) produced the durable Fayum mummy portraits of Graeco-Roman Egypt (c. 100–300 AD). See [[Media, Vehicles & Solvents]] and [[Supports & Materials]].
- **Culture ran the economy of pigment:** the lapis of Badakhshan moved along trade routes at currency-grade value (see [[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue]]), and Tyrian purple was so costly it became an imperial monopoly — the first color markets were markets in status, not in science.

### The Age of Oil & Vermeer — practice outruns theory

- the oil age shows the loop's two halves **converging without yet connecting** — the era's most refined material practice (Vermeer's studio) and its boldest physical speculation (Huygens on the composition of light) shared a town, not a framework.
- Oil painting was **not van Eyck's invention**: it developed gradually from at least the **13th century**, as in the Norwegian altar frontals painted with a drying-oil/egg mixture; by the 15th century Rogier van der Weyden (c. 1435) used **linseed oil** for unprecedented depth and realistic texture, and later research disproved the 1781 "van Eyck invents oil" legend. See [[Media, Vehicles & Solvents]].
- **Johannes Vermeer (1632–1675)** was a pinnacle of this technical evolution: scrupulous about materials, he repeatedly used the expensive natural ultramarine alongside yellow ochre, and his work shows an advanced mastery of light "edges" and diffraction — Carlson calls him the summit of understanding edges or halation in paint. His compositions have been read as "shimmering prismatic hypotheses".
- Tellingly, Vermeer paired blue and yellow at a time when his compatriot Christiaan Huygens theorized these as the two fundamental constituents of light — **material practice and optical theory arrived at the same two colors independently**. See [[Vermeer-Dutch Golden Age]].

### Scientific Revolution & the 19th-century synthetic boom — chemistry becomes the hinge

- Newton made the science of light public; Prussian blue made it **productive**. In one century chemistry manufactured the palette, and each new pigment in turn unlocked new painting — the loop's fastest turn yet.
- **New theories of light:** Newton's *Opticks* (1704) presented the first color circle, born from the 1666 prism demonstrations that white light is a mixture of seven spectral hues (handprint treats this circle as "the foundation image for artists' color wheels and the hue circle in all scientific color spaces"). Goethe's *Theory of Colours* (1810) reacted against Newton, insisting on the subjective, physiological experience of color, including colored shadows. See [[Newton's Influence on Painters]], [[Wave Nature]], [[The Visible Spectrum]], [[The artist's color wheel]], [[Color Psychology & Symbolism]].
- **The chemistry boom:** **[[PB27 - Prussian Blue|Prussian blue]]** (c. 1704, Diesbach) was the first documented synthetic pigment; it was followed by **[[PB28 - Cobalt Blue|cobalt blue]]** (1802), synthetic **[[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue|ultramarine]]** (1828, Guimet) — which collapsed the price of lapis from thousands of francs to tens, ending the economics of the ancient world's most precious blue — **[[PG18 - Viridian|viridian]]** (1838, Pannetier and Binet), and **[[PB35 - Cerulean Blue|cerulean blue]]** (1860, Rowney); the yellows and reds were transformed by **chromium yellows (c. 1803, Vauquelin)**, **[[PY35 - Cadmium Yellow|cadmium yellow]]** (discovered 1817, commercial 1840s), **[[PR83 - Alizarin Crimson|alizarin crimson]]** (1868) — the first natural colorant to be duplicated in the laboratory — and **[[PR108 - Cadmium Red|cadmium red]]** (c. 1910); **Perkin's mauve (1856)** was the first coal-tar colorant, founding the synthetic dyestuff industry. See [[Natural vs. Synthetic]], [[PY35 - Cadmium Yellow]], [[PR83 - Alizarin Crimson]], [[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue]], [[PB28 - Cobalt Blue]], [[PG18 - Viridian]], [[PB35 - Cerulean Blue]], [[PR108 - Cadmium Red]].
- **Impressionism & plein air:** James Rand's **collapsible paint tube (1841 patent)** freed Claude Monet and the Impressionists from the studio, enabling the **plein air** movement and the separation of the artist from manufacturing. The bright new synthetics let painters render shifting atmospheric light and literal **colored shadows** (blue shadows on snow), replacing the muted studio palette — **the supply of new pigments and the demand of new practice fed each other in a single generation**. See [[Impressionism]], [[Neo-Impressionism-Pointillism]], and [[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing]].

### The trickle-down of colorimetry — industry-born science returns to the studio

- the loop's defining instance — **color science was born of a factory problem and returned to art as a doctrine**. Chevreul's law was extracted from dyeing complaints at the Gobelins; a century later it governed how paintings were made.
- **Michel Eugène Chevreul (1786–1889),** chemist and director of dyeing at the Gobelins tapestry works, published the **Law of Simultaneous Contrast** (1839) — a "catechism of complementarity" that directly fed Vincent van Gogh and Georges Seurat. See [[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing]], [[Composition]], and [[Post-Impressionism]].
- **James Clerk Maxwell (1855)** established the **trichromatic** theory of color vision with his color triangle; **Ogden Rood**'s *Modern Chromatics* (1879) argued colors can be mixed **optically in the eye** through persistence of vision — spinning-disc mixtures that fed Seurat's Pointillism. See [[Color Matching Functions and the Photopic Luminosity Function]] and [[Optical vs. Physical Mixture]].
- **Albert H. Munsell (1858–1918),** American painter and teacher, replaced the wheel with the **color tree**, specifying color by **Hue, Value, and Chroma** on a scale of equal visual spacing — in *A Color Notation* (1905), later fixed numerically by the 1943 Munsell Renotation. See [[Munsell Notation]] and [[Why Lab-Munsell Were Built for Perceptual Uniformity]].
- **Standardization:** the **CIE 1931 standard observer** gave a mathematical frame for describing color, and **colorimetry** matured through the theoretical **optimal colors** of Erwin Schrödinger, the just-noticeable-difference **ellipses** and **MacAdam limits** of David MacAdam, and Michael Pointer's empirical survey of real surface colors (1980) — a genealogy detailed in "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits" and *Color for Science, Art, and Technology*. See [[CIE Systems]], [[MacAdam Ellipses]], [[Colors/Gamuts/Optimal Color Solid-MacAdam Limits|Optimal Color Solid-MacAdam Limits]], [[Pointer's Gamut]], [[Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red]].
- **Culture:** with CIE and Munsell, color was converted from private craft knowledge into a **public, measurable, standardized language** — the cultural act that let color leave the studio entirely (industry, imaging, design) and then return to the academy as curriculum.

### The present — the loop closed and self-documenting

- production, science, and preservation are now **one system**. The paint tube is a legible instrument, the museum is a laboratory, and pigment chronology itself has become a forensic clock — art history now runs on color science.
- Today the palette is dominated by **synthetic organic pigments** unmatched in intensity and permanence: the **phthalocyanines** (blue 1935, green 1938), the **quinacridones** (e.g. "Acra" red shades) and the **pyrroles**. See [[High-Chroma Synthetics]], [[PB15 - Phthalo Blue]], [[PG7 - Phthalo Green]], [[PR122 - Quinacridone Magenta]], [[PR254 - Pyrrole Red]].
- Paint tubes are now legible instruments: **Colour Index (CI) generic names** such as **[[PB27 - Prussian Blue|PB27 (Prussian blue)]]**, constitution numbers, **lightfastness ratings** and overdose label conformances turn the palette into a documented archive. See [[Transparency, Opacity & Pigment Codes]], [[Pigment Sources, Composition & Toxicity Reference]].
- Archival practice rests on technical examination — **X-ray** that reveals hidden revisions (**pentimenti**) and **infrared (IR) reflectography** that peers through paint layers to the original charcoal underdrawings, reading pigments, ground and condition without touching the paint film — and because modern pigments have known introduction dates (cadmium red c. 1910, titanium white in the 20th century), **pigment analysis is now a dating and forgery-detection tool**: the palette that once served ritual now serves evidence (see [[Pigments/Sourcing Real Spectral Data|Sourcing Real Spectral Data]], [[PW6 - Titanium White]]).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy frames the last three centuries as "truly amazing advances in pigment chemistry" — his recommended history being Philip Ball's *Bright Earth: The Art and Invention of Color* *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*. He stresses that **primary pigments are a moving target set by manufacturing**: Le Blon's early-1700s subtractive printing system used a carmine/madder-lake/vermilion red plus yellow lake and iron blue (PB27); Moses Harris's wheel of 1772 used vermilion, orpiment and natural ultramarine; Johann Lambert in 1785 preferred gamboge, carmine and iron blue; Charles Winter's mixing triangle used alizarin crimson, cadmium lemon and phthalo blue; a contemporary painter would choose benzimidazolone yellow, quinacridone magenta and phthalo cyan. The "true primary red" thus marched from vermilion (PR106) and madder (NR4), to alizarin crimson (PR83) in the late 19th century, to the modern recommendation of quinacridone (PR122/PV19) — all of them far from the ideal magenta hue, and the "primary blue" has only traveled about halfway toward ideal cyan *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*. MacEvoy also flags Newton's 1704 circle as the ancestor of every artists' wheel and scientific hue circle, but one that entangled three unresolved problems — the diatonic musical spacing, the additive "measurable light mixtures" it was invented to explain, and a self-defeating notion of primaries that made material and radiant color appear identical; painters who stuffed RYB into the circle made additive and subtractive mixing look like the same thing. On the chemistry itself, he notes the synthetic organics were a major second-half-of-the-19th-century innovation but many of the first coal-tar colorants proved too impermanent for art, while the modern 20th-century synthetics are far more durable — "with very few exceptions, all commercial artists' paints use synthetic pigments" today, and the Society of Dyers and Colourists publishes the Colour Index as the standard naming and description system for both dyes and pigments *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs compresses the intellectual arc into the **"late 19th century Helmholtz–Maxwell–Hering revolution"**: modern colour theory was settled then, and today's revival of traditional colour theory is "not based on a successful challenge to the truth of modern colour theory" but on art education's concurrent retreat from anatomy, perspective and drawing — its relationship to modern theory being "in some ways like that of so-called 'Creation Science' to modern biology": not science-versus-art, but "the sciences of different eras" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/113.md|113.html]])*. Tracing the primaries themselves, Briggs roots the painter's "mix everything from three" premise in **Aristotle's *Meteorologica* (c. 350 BC)** — whose three were red, green and blue-violet — with red/yellow/blue established among painters and dyers by the first decades of the 17th century and the first hue circle of artists' paints appearing in the anonymous 1708 Hague pastel manual; **Newton proved the "intermixture model" untenable** as physics (every hue except purple can be either simple or compound light), but left the puzzle of *why three* colourants are optimal, a gulf filled until **Helmholtz (1852, 1866)** and **Young** located the answer in three overlapping cone sensitivities rather than in three "rays" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/062.md|062.html]])*.
