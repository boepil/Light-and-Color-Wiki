**Scope:** Cross-cutting page (Painting × Eye) covering perceptual phenomena critical for painters.

The perception of color is not a passive recording of light wavelengths but an active, context-dependent construction by the visual system. Key phenomena — **simultaneous contrast**, **color constancy**, and **afterimages** — reveal the underlying biological mechanisms of the eye and brain; understanding the difference between **additive and subtractive mixing** is essential for mastering these effects in painting and digital media.

### 1. Simultaneous Contrast

**Simultaneous contrast** is the phenomenon where a color's appearance is altered by adjacent or surrounding colors. It was famously codified in **1839 by Michel-Eugène Chevreul**, a French chemist who realized that the perception of a color's purity, value, and hue is relative to its environment:

- **Mechanism:** driven by the **[[Opponent-Process Color Coding|opponent-process mechanism]]** and **lateral inhibition**. In the retina and cortex, "double opponent" cells compare signals from neighboring regions; when a target is surrounded by a strong color, the background effectively "induces" its own **complement** into the target.
- **Visual transformations:**
  - **Hue shift:** a neutral gray patch on a red background takes on a greenish (cyan) cast; the same gray on green appears reddish (magenta).
  - **Value shift:** a mid-value color appears lighter on a dark background and darker on a light background.
  - **Saturation/vibration:** two complementary colors of equal value placed edge-to-edge cause an "apparitional shimmer" or **visual vibration**, because the eye cannot simultaneously process both at rest.

### 2. Color Constancy

**Color constancy** is the ability to perceive an object's color as constant despite changes in the spectral composition of the light source:

- **Mechanism:** the brain achieves this through **chromatic adaptation**, sometimes described as "discounting the illuminant." Using heuristics — such as assuming the highest luminance in a scene is "white" — the brain factors out the light source's color to recover the object's true **reflectance spectrum**.
- **Limits of constancy:** constancy is robust under natural daylight but falters under **monochromatic or highly biased light sources**. Under orange sodium street lamps, blue objects appear black or gray because there is no blue light for the S-cones to detect, rendering the "discounting" mechanism ineffective. Constancy is not innate — it develops in infants between **two and four months of age**.

### 3. Afterimages

**Afterimages** (specifically negative afterimages) are fleeting false images seen after staring at an intense hue:

- **Mechanism:** they result from **retinal and neural fatigue** within the opponent-process channels. Staring at a red stimulus for **30–60 seconds** fatigues the L-cone pathway; when the gaze shifts to a neutral white surface (which contains all wavelengths), the fatigued red receptors respond weakly while the rested green and blue receptors respond at full strength.
- **Complementary relationships:** the afterimage is always the **complement** of the original stimulus:
  - Red → **cyan/green** afterimage;
  - Blue → **orange/yellow** afterimage;
  - Yellow → **blue/violet** afterimage.
- **Biological protection:** **saccadic eye movements** (involuntary micro-tremors) act like a "screen saver" for the retina, constantly shifting the image to prevent receptors from becoming permanently "burned in."

### 4. Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing

Mastering the difference between these two systems is critical for any visual artist:

- **Additive mixing (light):** the **superimposition of light rays** (RGB) — how computer monitors and stage lights work. Primaries: **Red, Green, Blue**. The more light added, the **lighter** the result; mixing the three primaries at 100% intensity yields **white**.
- **Subtractive mixing (pigment):** the **absorption of wavelengths** by physical matter. Primaries: **Cyan, Magenta, Yellow (CMY)**. Every added pigment subtracts more light, making the result **darker** and more "broken"; mixing the three primaries ideally yields **black**.
- **Optical mixture:** **[[Neo-Impressionism-Pointillism|Neo-Impressionists]]** like Seurat used **spatial additive mixing** — small dots of pure pigment merging in the eye — achieving higher **luminosity and saturation** than physical palette blending.

### 5. Interactive Effects in Painting Practice

A painter must account for these phenomena to maintain control over a composition:

- **Environmental influence:** because color is relative, an artist must mix a color not for what it is on the palette (the **agent**), but for how it will look in context (the **effect**). A color that looks "right" in isolation may "pop out" or look muddy once surrounded by neighbors.
- **Lighting shifts:** due to **[[Natural Light Gamut vs. Pigment Gamut - Metamerism|metamerism]]**, colors that match under studio lights may clash in a gallery. Pure colors also change value with illumination intensity — reds and yellows darken more rapidly than blues at twilight.
- **Managing fatigue:** prolonged work on a high-chroma passage can cause **retinal fatigue**, making the painter misperceive subsequent colors as duller than they are. A **neutral gray ground** or mask helps "reset" the eye for accurate comparison.

### 6. Concrete Demonstrations

**The afterimage experiment:**
1. **Preparation:** place a highly saturated red circle on a white background.
2. **Fixation:** stare intensely at a small dot in the center for **40 seconds**.
3. **The shift:** quickly move your eyes to a black dot on a clean white surface.
4. **Observation:** a "ghostly" **cyan** or pale green circle appears.

**Simultaneous contrast examples:**
- **The Jasper Johns Flag:** in *Flag*, Johns used a green, black, and yellow version of the American flag; after staring at it, the viewer perceives a negative afterimage of the flag in its "correct" **red, white, and blue** colors.
- **Gray carpet illusion:** a neutral gray carpet in a room with violet walls appeared **yellow**, because the eye automatically induced violet's complement into the gray.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's own afterimage color-matching experiments reveal that afterimages are **not equivalent around the hue circle**: a saturated blue does not produce an intense yellow afterimage in isolation, while a dull yellow can produce a relatively saturated violet; a saturated blue-green yields a relatively dull red, but a saturated red yields an intense blue-green. When colors are presented together, mutual influence sharpens the hue contrasts — revealing the powerful effect of context even on afterimages. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*

He also notes that bright-light **positive afterimages** — e.g., the yellow-green phantom seen after a camera flash in a dim room — oscillate through hues as the bleached rods and cones recover at different rates, lagging the chromatic adaptation mechanisms of the visual cortex. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*

> [!WARNING] **The Color Wheel Fallacy (Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing).** MacEvoy argues that applying a geometrically perfect color wheel to paint mixing is a fundamental error. The traditional wheel (and uniform spaces like CIELAB) successfully models *additive light mixing*, where lights can be brightened or dimmed without altering mixture color. But **paint mixing is subtractive, bound by multiplicative reflectance overlap** — mixing two paints multiplies their reflectance curves, which does not behave with geometric symmetry, and paint chroma changes unpredictably when diluted. Opponent-color theory and geometric wheels are grounded in additive-light physics and eye biology; they do not — and mathematically cannot — accurately predict the physical reality of subtractive paint mixing. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color14.md|color14.html]])*
