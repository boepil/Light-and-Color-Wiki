**Scope:** How the physics of light falloff, the inverse-square law, the form principle, edge control, and secondary ambient illumination govern value gradients and accentuate local color chroma in representational painting.

### 1. Physics of Light Falloff & The Inverse-Square Law

The intensity of light reaching an object surface is governed by the **inverse-square law**:

$$E = \frac{I}{r^2}$$

where $E$ is illuminance, $I$ is source intensity, and $r$ is the distance from a point or small light source (*Carlson's Guide to Landscape Painting*, "Light"; *vision-science-photons-to-pheno*, §1.2.1). This physical relationship dictates the rate of value falloff across a painted scene:

- **Steep vs. Gradual Falloff:** When an object or figure sits close to a small light source ($r$ is small, e.g. 1 m to 2 m), illuminance drops by **75%** over just one meter. This produces a **steep falloff** — dramatic value contrast, compressed highlights, and rapid transition into deep shadow (*Carlson's Guide*; *Itten*, *The Art of Color*). Moving the source farther away ($r$ is large, e.g. 10 m to 11 m) drops illuminance by only **~17%** over the same distance, producing **gradual falloff** and uniform, soft value gradients across the form.
- **Studio Light Dynamics:** In the studio, a single candle or unshielded incandescent bulb acts as a point source, casting sharp-edged, high-contrast shadows with steep falloff — famously exploited by **Georges de La Tour** (e.g. *The Newborn Babe*, c. 1450) to isolate forms against pitch darkness (*Itten*). Conversely, a large north window or ground-glass door acts as an **extended light source**, diffusing light across the room and creating luminous, gentle falloff with soft, wide shadow borders (*Carlson's Guide*; *vision-science-photons-to-pheno*).

### 2. The Form Principle & Peak Chroma in the Halftone

Light hitting a three-dimensional form organizes its surface into six canonical value zones (*Carlson's Guide*; *Devin Korwin Creative Fundamentals*, Ch. 3):

1. **Specular Highlight:** Direct mirror reflection of the light source.
2. **Halftone (Midtone):** Planes turning toward the light at an oblique angle.
3. **Form Shadow:** Planes turning away from the light source.
4. **Core Shadow:** The darkest band of the form shadow where light is most completely excluded.
5. **Reflected Light:** Secondary light bounced back onto the shadow side from neighboring surfaces.
6. **Cast Shadow & Penumbra:** Darkness projected onto adjacent surfaces, bordered by a soft transition zone.

#### Why Peak Chroma Lives in the Halftone
A foundational rule of color dynamics is that **local color and peak chroma reside in the halftone, NOT in the highlight or the deep shadow** (*Korwin*, Ch. 3; *Itten*). 
- **The Specular Highlight** represents intense, unabsorbed light glare that "whitens" and desaturates the surface — as Leonardo da Vinci observed, the highest light is a "privation of color" (*Itten*).
- **The Deep Shadow** lacks sufficient photon flux to stimulate the eye's L, M, and S cone channels, obscuring the hue into near-blackness.
- **The Halftone** receives moderate, directional illumination that allows the pigment's selective spectral absorption to operate fully without being overwhelmed by specular glare or starved by darkness. Here the form turns toward the light, displaying the pigment's true, unadulterated chroma (*Korwin*; *Carlson's Guide*).

#### Value Grouping ("The Oreo Cookie Principle")
To prevent value "mud" and preserve clear color identity, painters group values strictly: maintaining one unified value range for the shadow mass, a controlled gradient across the halftones, and a sharp accent for specular highlights (*Korwin*). Keeping shadows and lights in distinct value families prevents value overlap from destroying the illusion of 3D form.

### 3. Chiaroscuro, Tenebrism & Sfumato

Painters have historically manipulated falloff and value contrast to heighten the perceived resonance of local colors:

- **Chiaroscuro (Leonardo da Vinci & Rembrandt):** The distribution of light and dark to model 3D volume. **Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519)** pioneered *sfumato* ("smokiness"), using infinitesimal tonal gradations without harsh outlines so that forms merge imperceptibly into shadow (*Itten*). **Rembrandt (1606–1669)** used chiaroscuro to make saturated focal colors shine "like jewels in dull settings," constructing portraits (*Man in a Golden Helmet*) as a fabric of darks punctuated by small, intense highlights (*Itten*).
- **Tenebrism (Caravaggio & Gentileschi):** Extreme, dramatic chiaroscuro where darks dominate the canvas. **Caravaggio (1571–1610)** painted on dark red-brown grounds, laying in pitch-black shadow masses first and allowing impasted lights to emerge as vivid, isolated focal "spots" out of the darkness (*Goethe*, *Theory of Colours*; *Korwin*). The steep falloff pins high-chroma accents into pools of light, maximizing visual punch.

### 4. Colored Shadows, Ambient Fill & Reflected Light

Shadows are rarely neutral grays or blacks; they are lit by secondary environmental light sources (*Contemporary Color*, Ch. 4; *Illusions of Seeing*, §5.5.1):

- **Sky Fill & Rayleigh Scattering:** Outdoors under direct sunlight, the sun acts as a warm point source, while the entire dome of the sky acts as a vast, secondary blue source due to **Rayleigh scattering** ($1/\lambda^4$ short-wavelength scattering) (*Illusions of Seeing*). Consequently, planes in shadow that face upward "see" the sky and absorb cool blue or violet light (*Korwin*; *Carlson's Guide*).
- **Warm Key vs. Cool Shadow Inversion:** A warm light source (e.g. golden late-afternoon sun or 2800 K tungsten) produces cool blue-violet shadows, whereas a cool light source (e.g. 10,000 K north skylight) produces warm ochre-brown shadows — a phenomenon driven by visual adaptation and the eye's opponent contrast (*Bleicher*; *Carlson's Guide*). Vincent van Gogh systematically exploited this in *Night (after Millet)*, pairing warm firelight with deep blue shadows (*Bleicher*).
- **Ambient Occlusion & Reflected Bounces:** **Ambient occlusion** is the localized darkening in tight crevices, cracks, and contact points where neither key light nor sky fill can penetrate (*Korwin*). Conversely, downward-facing shadow planes catch warm **reflected light** bounced from the ground or tabletop, saturating the shadow core with environmental color (*Korwin*; *vision-science-photons-to-pheno*).

### 5. Edge Control, Penumbra & Halation

The physical size of the light source determines shadow edge quality (*Korwin*; *Carlson's Guide*):

- **Point Source vs. Extended Source:** A small point source produces hard, crisp shadow edges. A large extended source (overcast sky, softbox) creates a wide **penumbra** — the soft boundary zone of a cast shadow where the light source is only partially occluded (*Korwin*).
- **Form Shadows vs. Cast Shadows:** Form shadows are inherently softer than cast shadows because the physical surface rolls away from the light gradually. Cast shadow edges grow progressively softer as the distance between the casting object and the receiving surface increases.
- **Vermeer, Diffraction & Halation:** **Johannes Vermeer** was a supreme master of edge mechanics. He exploited **diffraction** (the bending of light around opaque edges) and **halation** — the visual effect where intense light suffuses or "overflows" over dark overlapping borders (such as dark lace or foliage against a sunlit wall) (*Carlson's Guide*; *color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf*, §4.7.3). Halation dissolves hard edges into luminous "atmospheric edges," enhancing the perceived brilliance of adjacent light areas.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy establishes **value as the single dominant element of visual contrast** in painting *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color11.md|color11.html]])*. Value defines spatial structure, form, and focal hierarchy; stripping chromaticity from an image leaves its structural meaning intact, whereas stripping value differences collapses the composition entirely.

On light falloff and color shifts, MacEvoy notes that **light intensity shifts apparent hue toward yellow as illuminance increases**: blues shift to blue-green, greens to yellow-green, oranges to yellow, and reds to orange *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]])*. In shadowed or filtered light, the shift reverses toward red or blue-violet. Daylight shadows are naturally tinted with a desaturated red-blue (such as indanthrone blue or iron blue) *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]])*.

Furthermore, Gilchrist's **lightness anchoring theory** demonstrates that human vision anchors "white" to the highest luminance in a spatial context *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color11.md|color11.html]])*. Painters do not paint absolute luminance (which can span 1:10,000 outdoor lux); they paint **relative spatial lightness contrast**, using local edge relationships to make a shadowed white paper look white even if its physical light output is darker than a sunlit black velvet.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs maps form lighting into 3D color spaces (HSB / CIELAB) *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/013.md|013.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/015.md|015.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/092.md|092.html]])*. As light intensity falls across a turning form, the surface location traces a downward trajectory in color space: $L^*$ (lightness) and $C^*$ (chroma) decrease simultaneously toward the dark origin. Because deep shadow reduces both $L^*$ and $C^*$, **high chroma is physically impossible in shadow masses** without adding an emissive light source.

Briggs also addresses the **"lightness of shadows" in representational painting** *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/093.md|093.html]])*. Artists operate on a compressed physical reflectance scale ($L^* \approx 20$ to $90$, a contrast ratio of barely 1:30 to 1:100). To evoke the brilliant light falloff of nature, painters rely on relative value stepping and ambient shadow fills rather than attempting to match physical outdoor illuminance.
