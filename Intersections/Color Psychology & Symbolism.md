**Scope:** Cross-cutting page (Painting × Eye) on how color acquires emotional, symbolic, and commercial power — the evidence quality behind those claims, the cultural relativity of symbolism, Kandinsky's synesthetic doctrine, and the warm/cool tradition.

### The Evidence Landscape: Doctrine vs. Experiment vs. Anecdote
Sources sort claims about color's psychological effects into three tiers; most popular beliefs live in the weakest tiers.

- **Controlled studies:** red light has been reported to raise heart rate, blood pressure, and respiration in some clinical observations — but **more recent controlled studies using patternless monochromatic light failed to replicate** significant cardiovascular change, suggesting residual "arousal" is a cognitive response to symbolic meaning rather than direct physiology.
- **Surveys and clinical trials:** subjects consistently assign blue to "sympathy" and "trust", and tests show it can lower skin temperature and pulse. A 2023 pilot study with fibromyalgia patients found green-tinted glasses significantly reduced painkiller use; children in green rooms reported less homesickness.
- **Anecdotes:** blue lights kept mink ranchers' vicious animals calm; dark red cars are said to be over-represented in fatal accidents — possibly because the color attracts less cautious drivers.
- **The pink cell:** the U.S. Navy painted holding cells pink and hostile sailors turned docile — but after roughly **15 minutes** of exposure they became *more* aggressive and violent; a rare color-mood effect with a documented dose window.
- **Focal colors:** subjects name focal colors (primary yellow, etc.) fastest in color-naming tasks — a hint of universal biological prototypes beneath the cultural symbolism.

### Cultural Variation: Symbols Are Local, Vision Is Universal
Color *vision* is biologically universal; color *significance* is learned and almost always local and contextual.

- **White:** purity and innocence in the West (wedding gowns); in **China, Japan, and many African nations** white is the color of death and mourning, worn at funerals to honor the purity of the departed soul.
- **Red:** danger or love in the West (Valentine's Day); in **China and India** a joy-bringing hue of good luck, wealth, and marriage — the traditional wedding color; in parts of **Africa** red connects to death (Botswana replaced the red AIDS ribbon with a green one for a more "life-affirming" message of hope).
- **Blue:** honesty and authority in Western tradition (police uniforms); in **China** an "ugly" color of bad character or torment; in **India** historically avoided by high-caste Hindus because its fermentation process was considered impure.
- **Yellow:** in **Imperial China** the royal color, reserved exclusively for the emperor (supreme wisdom); in **modern China** it lost that "aura" and now reads as industrial machinery safety yellow. 
- Gage's art-historical rule (as relayed by Handprint): the surest antidote to dogmatic color theory is art history — **no consistent color meanings exist in world art** *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*.

### Kandinsky's Synesthetic Doctrine
Kandinsky's color theory is presented in the sources as **deeply personal, artistic, and spiritual doctrine — not empirical science**. He believed colors speak directly to the **soul of the beholder**, bypassing culture.

- **Color–sound maps:** yellow → shrill trumpet or canary's high trill; blue → deep violin, bass, or wind tones; vermilion red → the impression of "loud beats of the drum".
- **Color–form maps:** fixed pairings claimed as **universally shared** — yellow = triangle (aggressive, earthly energy); blue = circle (spiritual, concentric depth); red = square (stable, grave matter). His **[[Bauhaus|Bauhaus]]** colleagues disagreed.
- **"Pseudo-synesthete"** describes him because it is unclear whether the cross-sensory responses were truly involuntary. He rejected simple learned links (yellow is "sour" because of lemons), arguing instead that colors set up **"vibrations in the soul"** that then echo across other sensory organs, from taste to touch.

### Warm/Cool: Physics, Learned Association, and a ~200-Year Convention
The warm/cool axis bundles three separable strands.

- **Physical layer:** long wavelengths (red/orange) are transformed into stretching/bending vibrations of chemical bonds, sensed organically like an infrared lamp; short wavelengths (blue/violet) carry higher energy per photon but produce no such thermal vibration in matter. A controlled study reported a **5–7 degree** subjective temperature difference between rooms painted blue-green versus red-orange.
- **Learned layer:** John F. Carlson's associationist explanation — we psychologically associate red/yellow with fire (warm) and blue/white with ice (cold) — which MacEvoy demolishes in the Handprint Perspectives section.
- **Convention layer:** the modern warm/cool "buckets" (e.g., red-violet = warm, yellow-green = cool) were formalized **about 200 years ago** and have barely changed in art education since; abstract categories carried as if they were discoveries. They also carry an emotional payload: warm tones → proximity, safety, intimacy; cool tones → distance, space, the unknown — why sci-fi cinema color-grades blue and "childhood intimacy" scenes use warm pastels.
- **Schopenhauer's proportion principle:** balance demands small quantities of high-value (yellow) against large regions of low-value (violet).

### Hue, Value, and Chroma: The Conflation Problem
Many psychology studies report "color" effects while actually varying value or chroma — the three axes this wiki keeps separate.

- **The dockworkers' story:** dark containers were perceived as too heavy; repainted a *lighter-value* hue, the same boxes were accepted — the lightness axis alone.
- **Chroma arousal:** high-chroma red or blue rooms trigger galvanic skin response and quicker heart rate; low-chroma versions of the same hues calm and slow muscle reaction time.
- **Value and form:** **[[Color Field|Ad Reinhardt's]]** "black paintings" — closely-valued, low-contrast fields that force prolonged fixation — reveal changing color and form through **[[Simultaneous Contrast, Color Constancy, Afterimages, Subtractive vs. Additive Mixing|simultaneous contrast]]**, with barely any hue content at all.

### Appetite, Retail, and Branding: Associationism at Work
- **Appetite:** fast-food chains (McDonald's, Burger King) use red, orange, yellow because they believe the hues **increase appetite**; **blue** is the suppressant — rare in natural foods, it makes patrons eat less and feel full faster.
- **Flavor–color coupling:** color shifts perceived taste — beverages need their expected caramel coloring or consumers can't identify the taste.
- **Trust and urgency:** Facebook, IBM, and Chase Bank use blue to "whisper trust" (safe skies, calm water); red price tags exploit the fast nervous-system reaction to red — urgency.
- **Class-shaped palettes:** research suggests higher socioeconomic status groups favor darker, less saturated, complex hues, while those in lower brackets respond more favorably to bright, pure, primary hues.
- **Evidence base:** some physiological recordings (red-arousal, blue-calming) are described as "reliably recorded," but John Gage notes market-research conclusions often remain "stranded in the circular arguments of opinion polls"; chromotherapy and many branding claims have a weak empirical base next to core vision science.

## Handprint Perspectives
Bruce MacEvoy's viewpoint. *(Sources: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*

**Depth and mood effects are lightness/chroma, not hue.** The traditional schemata — "warm colors advance and arouse, cool colors recede and calm" — are art-nursery "fictions": "ice is blue, so blue seems cool," or (his favorite) "a distant mountain appears blue, so blue recedes from us." The counterexamples are obvious once pointed out: in landscapes the blue sky overhead appears much closer than the whitish horizon — why doesn't blue advance? On a hot noon the blue sky *radiates more heat* than the reddish sunset — why is blue then "cold"? The real drivers are **lightness and chroma**: warm palette pigments as a group reach higher chroma and lighter value than their cool complements (ultramarine the rare exception). Match the value/chroma of a warm color to its cool complement — turn bright yellow to raw umber with black — and the "advancing, cheerful, attention-getting" effect dies; nobody says brown is advancing or exciting. And comparing monochromatic lights removes the pigment-chroma confound entirely: **violet light is the most saturated and appears the most advancing/arousing** of all, while yellow light looks bland — the reverse of the paint-store stereotype. MacEvoy concludes the depth/mood effects were grafted onto the warm/cool contrast by late 18th and 19th century "color theorists" and had no part in the Baroque landscape practice it grew from.

**Universal color-emotion research did not survive contact with evidence.** From ~1890 onward, academic color psychology tested the hypothesis that colors have universal physiological/psychological meaning — including Bauhaus questionnaires sent out to measure collective emotional associations of colors. The net result: "hundreds of publications showing that consistent physiological effects of color don't exist"; the one well-established finding being the beneficial effect of white light exposure in treating seasonal affective disorder (SAD). When hue, lightness, and chroma are controlled separately, **lightness and chroma dominate, and hue in isolation is the weak channel**. Culture attaches its own symbolic associations to colors — Gage's proof from art history: no consistent color meanings exist, and movements like the Pre-Raphaelites, Cubists, and Fauves deliberately broke conventional color codes. Any appeal to "universal" meaning must survive this record; the only well-established therapeutic case is white light for SAD.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag:** The notebook corpus reports physiological color-effects experiments (red arousal, the 5–7° room studies, chroma/galvanic-skin findings) and marketing claims; Handprint insists the controlled literature fails to replicate except as lightness/chroma artifacts — "consistent physiological effects of color don't exist." Even the corpus itself concedes part of this (Query 1's red-light finding "failed to replicate"). The underlying agreement: residual effects are carried by *meaning* and by relative lightness/chroma, not by hue alone. The remaining friction is attribution — the notebook's claims lean on the symbolic value of hue, while Handprint attributes the effects to value/chroma, leaving hue as the weak channel. *(Sources: "Color and Meaning" — John Gage, "Contemporary Color" — Steven Bleicher vs. [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]] / [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

David Briggs deconstructs the warm/cool tradition and the psychological claims surrounding hue categories:

- **Historical origin and extreme ambiguity of "Warm/Cool":** Hue-temperature associations were unknown in antiquity and the Renaissance, appearing in print only after Newton's spectral circle — first recorded in a 1727 German lexicon and described by Robert Dossie (1758) as used "without any precise or clear meaning." Across 19th and 20th-century colour wheels (Goethe, Hayter, Ross, Hiler, Itten), the warm-cool boundary shifted wildly, leaving **6 to 8 hues out of 12 ambiguous**. For instance, the "warm blue" debate splits painters into two fiercely opposing camps: one calling reddish blue (ultramarine) warm and greenish blue (phthalo) cool, and the other asserting the exact opposite *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/077.md|077.html]])*.
- **The Conflation of Hue with Chroma and Value:** In classrooms, "make it warmer" is routinely used when a teacher actually means *increase chroma* or *shift hue*. Because warm palette pigments (cadmiums, iron oxides) reach higher chroma-lightness combinations than cool pigments, painters mistake high chroma and high lightness for "warmth" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/077.md|077.html]])*.
- **Chromostereopsis vs. Visual Insistence:** The perception that "warm colours advance and cool colours recede" stands on a real optical phenomenon — **chromostereopsis**, caused by the differing focal points of long- and short-wavelength light in stereoscopic vision. However, because chromostereopsis varies by individual and does not affect everyone, the primary driver of "advancing" colour in art is simply **high lightness and high chroma**, which make an area visually insistent regardless of hue *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/077.md|077.html]])*.
- **Colour Temperature Inversion:** In colour science, **colour temperature** (CIE blackbody radiator scale in Kelvin) is the exact *inverse* of art-school terminology: low Kelvin temperatures (2000–3000 K) correspond to reddish/yellowish light, while high Kelvin temperatures (10,000–20,000 K) correspond to blue sky light. Conflating painterly "warmth" with physical colour temperature creates severe technical confusion *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/077.md|077.html]])*.
- **Pedagogical Recommendation:** Because warm/cool hue labels cause immediate confusion in teaching, Briggs advocates replacing "warm blue / cool blue" with unambiguous descriptors like **"reddish blue" and "greenish blue"**, reserving "warm" and "cool" for near-neutral background colours or general atmospheric/expressive mood *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/077.md|077.html]])*.
