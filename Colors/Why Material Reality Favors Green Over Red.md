**Scope:** Why green, uniquely among hues, can be both bright and intensely saturated in real reflecting materials — and why red is locked into a "pure but dark, or light but dull" bind.

The empirical observation that green can achieve significantly higher chroma than red is a central paradox of color science. While the **1943 Munsell Renotation** data shows **Green (2.5G)** and **Green-Yellow (10GY)** reaching a chroma of **34**, **Red (5R)** peaks at only **20**. This "material supremacy" of green is not merely an accident of pigment manufacturing but a fundamental convergence of the eye's luminosity function, the geometry of color space, and the physical chemistry of solids. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*

### 1. The Luminosity Function: The "Green Window"

The primary reason green dominates in material reality is the **photopic luminosity function (V(λ))**, which describes the human eye's sensitivity to different wavelengths.

- **Peak sensitivity:** the human retina processes green most efficiently because its sensitivity peaks near **555 nm** — the green-yellow region. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*
- **Brightness vs. radiance:** green stimuli are inherently "bright" per unit radiance. A green surface can achieve high lightness (Value) while reflecting only a narrow, pure band of light aligned with that peak. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*
- **The red limitation:** in contrast, the eye's sensitivity is very low at long wavelengths (>600 nm) where red lives. To make a red pigment appear "light" (high Value), an artist must allow it to reflect more green and blue light, which immediately desaturates the hue and lowers its chroma. Red is thus forced into a **"physical bind": it can be pure but dark, or light but dull**. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*

### 2. The Asymmetry of the MacAdam Limits

The theoretical ceiling for color saturation is defined by the **MacAdam limits** (or optimal colors) — ideal reflecting surfaces with "step-function" reflectance (reflecting either 0% or 100% at each wavelength).

- **Geometric protrusion:** even with these "perfect" theoretical spectra, the color solid is asymmetric. The greatest gamut volume exists in the **green-yellow sector at high lightness levels**. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*
- **Pointer's Gamut:** this theoretical asymmetry is confirmed empirically by **Pointer's Gamut (1980)**, which measured over 4,000 real-world surfaces (textiles, flowers, minerals) and found that green and yellow-green consistently reach higher chroma than red at equivalent lightness levels. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*

### 3. Physical Chemistry and Pigment Structure

The molecular and structural properties of green pigments make them physically superior to reds at isolating "pure" color.

- **Sharp absorption edges:** green organic pigments, particularly **copper phthalocyanines**, possess exceptionally sharp absorption edges. They can be engineered to absorb blue and red light aggressively while leaving a narrow, high-reflectance "transmission window" in the green. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits", "The Material Supremacy of Green Chroma")*
- **Broad red transitions:** red pigments require a much broader absorption band (400–580 nm) to eliminate blue and green. This breadth makes it harder to maintain "steep" transitions, resulting in reflectance spectra that are "contaminated" by adjacent wavelengths. Even the best organic reds show "vibrational overtones" that desaturate the color. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits", "The Material Supremacy of Green Chroma")*
- **Scattering and IR leakage:** green pigments often exhibit efficient diffuse reflectance. Red pigments, however, frequently suffer from **near-infrared (IR) leakage** and forward scattering, which adds invisible energy that broadens the measured reflectance spectrum and mathematically reduces calculated chroma. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*

### 4. Evolutionary Tuning

The visual system evolved to exploit the abundance of green in the natural world.

- **Primate evolution:** humans have survived through 20 million years of primate evolution staring at leaves. Green represented resources, growth, and safety. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*
- **Neural efficiency:** because green sits in the exact middle of the visible spectrum, the retina processes it with a **lower physiological load**, leading the brain to interpret green environments as restful or "safe". *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*
- **Opponent sensitivity:** the **L and M cones**, which drive the red–green opponent channel, have overlapping sensitivities that peak in the yellow-green region. This makes the visual system exquisitely sensitive to small chromatic changes in green, even if the "red" end of the channel is theoretically just as important. *(Source: "Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red: The Physical Chemistry of Chromatic Limits")*

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's *warm/cool* analysis supplies the counterweight: green's edge is a **high-lightness** advantage, not an absolute record of chroma. He notes that "purple, blue or green hues contract toward the white point (become less saturated) as lightness increases, but for hues from yellow green through red the maximum chroma boundaries remain at maximum saturation, along the spectrum locus." The reason is the **"warm cliff" reflectance curve** — a sharp reflectance jump between "cyan" and "orange", high reflectance on the "red" side and low on the "blue" side — that characterizes all saturated red, orange and yellow paints; and "in the average chroma of different watercolor pigments... the highest available chroma appears in red to red orange hues." In MacEvoy's framing the physical facts run the *opposite* direction from the renotation's theoretical ceiling: among real paints it is the warm hues, not green, that most closely match their ideal optimal-color profile and reach chroma nearly equal to monochromatic lights. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color12.md|color12.html]])*

MacEvoy also puts the evolutionary-emotional gloss under suspicion. He explicitly rejects "universal physical or psychological reactions to different colors" — the claim that "green calms and red excites" has been tested by academic color research since about 1890 with "the net result... just hundreds of publications to show that consistent physiological effects of color don't exist." The "green = restful/safe" reading of chromatic limits is therefore a cultural interpretation layered onto a physical fact, not a proven physiological response. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18b.md|color18b.html]])*

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag:** The renotation's theoretical ceiling (green 34 vs red 20) and the real-pigment record (red-orange 16 vs cyan-green 10) point in opposite directions. Briggs's data shows "the highest chromas (16 Munsell chroma units) are attained in the hue range from orange-yellow to orange-red, while the lowest maximum chromas (10 units) are reached in the hue range from cyan to green" — and "none of our green or blue paints attain the near-optimal plateau-shaped reflectance curves seen in many red, orange and yellow paints." The reconciliation: green wins *at high lightness* (the luminosity function lets a bright green also be saturated), while red-orange wins *at maximum absolute chroma* (the warm-cliff profile stays at full saturation while greens and blues contract toward white as they lighten). Both statements are true of different regions of the same lopsided solid.
