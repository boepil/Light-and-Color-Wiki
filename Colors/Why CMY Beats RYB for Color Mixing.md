**Scope:** Why the CMY (cyan, magenta, yellow) subtractive primary set spans a wider mixing gamut than the traditional RYB (red, yellow, blue) triad — the physics of one-third-spectrum absorption aligned to the eye's three cones, spectral purity and band shape, the colors RYB physically cannot reach, and the history from Le Blon and Helmholtz to modern pigments.

### The physics of the one-third rule

The visible spectrum runs roughly **400–700 nm**, and the trick of subtractive mixing is absorption. An **ideal subtractive primary** absorbs **one-third of the visible spectrum** and reflects the other two-thirds — no more, no less. The CMY set is built exactly this way, and each member is complementary to one class of the eye's cone receptors.

- **Cyan** absorbs **red (≈600–700 nm)**, reflects blue and green.
- **Magenta** absorbs **green (≈500–600 nm)**, reflects red and blue.
- **Yellow** absorbs **blue (≈400–500 nm)**, reflects red and green.

Because these absorption windows line up with the three cone classes (L, M, S), a CMY mixer can **independently control the stimulation of each cone type** — the widest possible range of cone responses, hence the largest gamut. Cyan stimulates short *and* medium cones; magenta stimulates short *and* long cones; because the modern primaries stimulate **two cone classes each**, their mixtures retain more chroma than mixtures of the historical colors.

The RYB triad fails the same test. Its members are **spectrally "impure"**: a schoolbook red (such as **[[PR108 - Cadmium Red|Cadmium Red]]**) reflects red *and some green* light, making it a poor primary for absorbing a clean band; **[[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue|Ultramarine Blue]]** often reflects some red light as a violet undertone that scrambles clean green mixtures. More than one century of measurement confirms that **no pigment represents a single pure ray of the spectrum**, and the RYB pairs fail to unite in a "balanced neutrality," leaving an excess of muddy orange.

### Why RYB cannot reach cyan, magenta, or clean violets

The gap is not cosmetic — it is structural. In modern color science the RYB "primaries" turn out to be **secondaries**: red is close to yellow-plus-magenta, blue is close to cyan-plus-magenta. The direct consequences:

- **The magenta/cyan void.** You cannot mix magenta or cyan from red, yellow, and blue paints. Red and blue sit too far apart in hue, and their stray reflected wavelengths neutralize each other, producing a **muddy near-black instead of a vivid purple**.
- **Curved mixing paths.** Pigment mixing does not follow straight lines between the three primaries; it follows **curves on the chromaticity diagram**. CMY mixing paths **curve outward and retain chroma**; RYB paths **curve inward toward the neutral center** and rapidly lose saturation. A saturated red absorbs some orange light, so mixing it with yellow inevitably yields a duller, unsaturated orange.
- **Mixtures are always duller than their parts.** The "impure" nature of every pigment means paint mixtures are invariably muddier than a single pigment — the RYB set compounds the loss because its bands overlap in the middle of the spectrum.

### Spectral purity and band shape decide the ceiling

"Vividness" (chroma) is a measure of **spectral purity** — how sharply a pigment reflects its own wavelengths and suppresses everything else. Ideal pigments, the **[[Optimal Color Solid-MacAdam Limits|Schrödinger / MacAdam step pigments]]**, switch between **0% and 100% reflectance** at sharp transitions; the sharper the edge, the higher the chroma reachable at moderate value ([[Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red]]). Modern organic colorants approach this ideal:

- [[PB15 - Phthalo Blue]] — exceptionally sharp absorption edge, the other half of the cyan slot.
- [[PR122 - Quinacridone Magenta]] — high chroma at moderate/dark values, the magenta slot.
- [[PY97 - Hansa Yellow]] — permanent, high-strength, the light-yellow slot.

Green pigments (e.g., **[[PG7 - Phthalo Green|Phthalo Green PG7]]**) are easier to make sharp than reds, which need a broad absorption band to kill both blue and green and therefore leak shallow tails that reduce top chroma. This asymmetry is why the CMY gamut's cyan/green side is easier to expand than its red side — and it is the same physics that makes the RYB set's red such a poor primary.

### A practical translation to the palette

| Primary role | Modern CMY pigment | Traditional RYB pigment | Why the CMY pick wins |
|:----------- |:------------------------------------------------------------- |:---------------------------------------------------------- |:-------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Yellow | [[PY97 - Hansa Yellow\|Hansa Yellow (PY97)]] | [[PY35 - Cadmium Yellow\|Cadmium Yellow]] | clean ½-spectrum reflectance, high strength |
| Cyan/Blue | [[PB15 - Phthalo Blue\|Phthalo Blue (PB15:3)]] | [[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue\|Ultramarine Blue]] | near-step absorption edge; Ultramarine's red undertone muddies greens |
| Red/Magenta | [[PR122 - Quinacridone Magenta\|Quinacridone Magenta (PR122)]] | [[PR108 - Cadmium Red\|Cadmium Red Light]] | reflects red+blue cleanly; Cadmium Red leaks green |

MacEvoy's **[[The artist's color wheel|functional paint wheel]]** converges on exactly these three: **primary magenta — quinacridone magenta**, **light yellow**, and **primary cyan — phthalocyanine blue GS** (or phthalocyanine cyan); the traditional triad — red, yellow, and blue — is, in his words, **"obsolete and should not be taught"** *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*. He is equally blunt about *why* this triad and not the historical one: the modern reason is simply measured outputs — three paints that **mix every hue at the highest possible chroma**, given the restriction of three colorants and the cost-effective, lightfast pigments actually available *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*. In printing the identical logic produced CMY process inks.

### History: from painters to printers

- **Le Blon**: J.C. Le Blon worked with red, yellow, and blue (1703), then — while pioneering **four-color printing** (c. 1720) — recognized that **cyan and magenta** were needed for full color fidelity, and whose three-paint recipe was in fact "red (more precisely magenta), yellow, and blue (more precisely cyan)".
- **Helmholtz (1852)** gave the correct account of subtractive paint mixing and the **inverse relationship between additive and subtractive primaries**; from it follow the named optimal sets — *yellow, pink, seagreen* (Benson 1868), *yellow, magenta, cyan blue* (Hatt 1908), and the *Ives primaries* (1935) ([[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/062.md|062.html]]).
- The RYB triad is a **historical naming accident**: yellow, red, and blue were the names painters applied to the best colorants *before* it was realized that hues we **mentally experience as primary are not the optimal hues for colorant mixing** — "this is the point that has still not occurred to a lot of art and design teachers" ([[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/062.md|062.html]]). Its formalization in art education runs from 18th-century painters to **[[Bauhaus|Johannes Itten's]]** 12-part Bauhaus circle, and it survives mainly because it is easy to teach for basic pigment discipline.
- **[[Munsell Notation|Munsell]]** attacked the same doctrine decades earlier, attributing the teaching that "red, yellow, and blue are primary hues" to **Brewster's theory** and calling out the "mischief" it does.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's measured-color verdict is uncompromising: the traditional primary triad "**is obsolete and should not be taught**," and the functional paint wheel today rests on **magenta, light yellow, and cyan**. But he is equally candid about the limits of three primary paints, and his mixing studies add an honest footnote to the simple "CMY wins" story: a bare primary triad produces **lopsided results around the wheel** — "bright orange and scarlet mixtures, relatively dull green and blue green mixtures, and very dull purple and blue violet mixtures," because saturation costs grow for paints farther apart in hue. There is no artist's pigment more intense than a **[[High-Chroma Synthetics|phthalocyanine]]** for cyan or a **[[High-Chroma Synthetics|quinacridone]]** for magenta, so the choice of primary is forced; the practical dodge used by painters is not RYB but a **split-primary** extension that adds high-chroma pigments *around* the CMY core to stretch the warm and violet sectors *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: "CMY wins" is real but not a blank check.** The notebook sources state plainly that CMY is the scientifically correct subtractive set because it is engineered to the eye's cones and the spectrum. MacEvoy and Briggs accept that fully — and then both add the same caveat: three *good* CMY paints mix every hue only **"at moderate chroma"**, and measured mixtures are hue-dependently lopsided (color13's bright-orange/dull-violet asymmetry; the widely-available "warm" reds that extend the gamut but cannot *by themselves* give a large gamut). So the honest claim is: CMY is the *optimal* three-colorant core — strictly better than RYB — but a maximal studio gamut still takes more than three paints. The RYB-to-CMY lesson is about choosing the *right core*, not about getting something from nothing. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/062.md|062.html]])*
