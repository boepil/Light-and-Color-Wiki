**Scope:** Why every color wheel is a human-made ordering system rather than a discovery — eight historical wheels built on different, sometimes contradictory organizing principles (pigment mixing, light physics, perception, or aesthetics), and why their disagreement is the evidence that color classification is arbitrary.

### Classification is an arbitrary act

The starting fact is that **color classification is arbitrary**: colors have **no inherent boundaries and no natural order**. The wavelengths of light differ **gradually**, so the spectrum runs continuously from violet through blue, green, yellow, orange to red **without sharp transitions** between one hue and the next. Where "blue" ends and "green" begins is a decision, not a discovery. Consequently, the different color wheels in circulation are **not variations of a single truth** — they are **distinct models built on specific organizing principles**, which may be pigment mixing, light physics, human perception, or subjective aesthetics, and which are frequently contradictory. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*

- The spectrum is not a circle; the loop only appears because the two spectral ends are perceptually joined by the non-spectral purples and magentas. The circle is added by perception and convention, not given by physics. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/031.md|031.html]])*
- The real, measured color solid is **tree-like with an irregular margin**, because maximum attainable chroma varies with value and from hue to hue; "simpler systems arbitrarily treat the maximum chroma attainable for each hue as uniform, resulting in a circular 'colour wheel' and a cylindrical colour space." *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/011.md|011.html]])*

### The eight systems

Each system below is a **specific tool designed to solve a different problem**. Reading them together, the point is not that one is "right" — it is that each chooses a different property to organize around.

#### 1. Newton's Color Circle (1704)

**Creator:** Sir Isaac Newton, published in *Opticks* (1704). **Organizing principle:** additive light.

- Newton "rolled up" the **linear spectrum into a circle** to mathematically plot light mixtures, dividing it into **seven colors — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet** — based on an **analogy with the seven tones of a musical scale**. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- His circle is the ancestor of the modern chromaticity diagram: the colour of a light mixture falls at the "center of gravity" of its spectral components, and white light, which he placed at the center, is its neutral point. It established the basic laws of **additive color mixture**. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/071.md|071.html]])*
- **Limitations:** the circle was **asymmetrical** to reflect the different proportions of the spectral bands; the **musical analogy** (seven colors) is a mystical/arbitrary choice rather than pure visual data; and it does **not predict pigment mixtures** — Newton's additive rules were explicitly rejected by artists' wheels. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges; [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*

#### 2. The Traditional Artist's RYB Wheel

**Creator:** developed by 18th-century theorists (e.g., J.C. Le Blon, 1703) and later formalized by art educators such as **Johannes Itten**. **Organizing principle:** historical subtractive mixing — red, yellow, and blue as "unreducible" primaries from which all other colors theoretically mix. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*

- **Benefits:** the most common model in art education, a simple framework for teaching secondary and tertiary colors, and a useful "discipline" for painters using limited palettes. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** RYB is a **subtractive approximation** less accurate than modern CMY science; its gamut is limited, especially in the **cyan, green, and violet** ranges; and it **fails to identify true perceptual complements** — red's actual complement is **blue-green**, not green. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges; [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]])*
- MacEvoy states it without hedging: the traditional primary triad "is obsolete and should not be taught"; a functional paint wheel today rests on magenta, light yellow, and cyan. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*

#### 3. Goethe's Color Theory/Wheel (1810)

**Creator:** Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, *Theory of Colours* (1810). **Organizing principle:** psychophysiological perception.

- Goethe organized colors around the **"demands" of the eye** — such as colored afterimages — and the **polarity between light and darkness**, rather than around the physics of light. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Benefits:** it foregrounds the **subjective experience** of color — emotional associations and the phenomenon of **colored shadows** — and deeply influenced the Impressionist painters. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** Goethe rejected Newton's spectral analysis, viewing it as "absurd" because it did not align with direct sensory experience; his model was also hindered by the flawed belief that color results from light mixing with a "darkening medium." *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- Goethe's opponent-pair structure (yellow–blue primaries, red–green) nonetheless anticipated Hering's opponent-process theory — an ironic afterlife for a theory whose physics was wrong. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/073.md|073.html]])*

#### 4. Itten's 12-Part Color Wheel (Bauhaus)

**Creator:** Johannes Itten, taught at the Bauhaus in the 1920s. **Organizing principle:** aesthetic pigment organization.

- Itten's wheel is a **flattened representation of a color sphere**, structured around the **RYB primary triad**, creating a highly ordered, geometric relationship between hues that he linked to **specific primary forms** (e.g., red square, yellow triangle). *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** it inherits the scientific inaccuracies of the RYB model, including a **"false color balance"**; critics argue it treats color **in a vacuum**, ignoring psychological, functional, or historical contexts. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- Briggs' verdict is far harsher: the post-Bauhaus *Art of Color* is "fabulously anachronistic," a version of colour theory "at least seventy years before the founding of the Bauhaus," and its teaching is the "colour theory equivalent of so-called 'creation science.'" *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*

#### 5. The Munsell Hue Circle (1905)

**Creator:** Albert Henry Munsell, *A Color Notation* (1905). **Organizing principle:** perceptual uniformity.

- Munsell organized color along **three independent dimensions — Hue, Value (lightness), and Chroma (saturation)** — and was the first to acknowledge that color perception is "chaotic" and does not fit a regular shape; the system forms an irregular **"color tree"** in which **different hues reach maximum chroma at different values**. It remains a **global standard** for industry and manufacturing. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** being three-dimensional, it is **difficult to represent on a single flat wheel** for studio use, and it offers **no direct instructions for mixing specific pigments**. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- Briggs: the concept of absolute chroma, the key of the system, "was devised by the American artist and art teacher, Albert Munsell," and the hue/value/chroma framework "has proved especially useful to painters." *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/011.md|011.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/112.md|112.html]])*

#### 6. CIE-Based Hue Circles (CIELAB)

**Creator:** Commission Internationale de l'Eclairage (1931; updated 1976). **Organizing principle:** additive light and cone sensitivity.

- CIE colorimetry uses **mathematical algorithms to match color stimuli to the responses of the human eye's photoreceptors**, providing a **device-independent "universal translator"** for color — precise matching across monitors, printers, and materials. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** the coordinate systems (L\*a\*b\*) are **highly abstract and mathematical**, unintuitive for traditional painters who think in terms of pigment names. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- The quasi-opponent CIE L\*a\*b\* system spaces hues perceptually while keeping an opponent red–green / yellow–blue axis structure — a descendant of both Newton's circle and Hering's axes. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/073.md|073.html]])*

#### 7. RGB (Additive) and CMY/CMYK (Subtractive) Wheels

**Creator:** RGB stems from early color science; CMY was pioneered by **J.C. Le Blon** for four-color printing. **Organizing principle:** device-based production.

- RGB is organized around red, green, and blue **light** for electronic displays; CMY is organized around cyan, magenta, and yellow for subtractive **print/ink** processes. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **CMY primaries are superior to RYB**: each absorbs **exactly one-third of the visible spectrum**, allowing a much **wider mixing gamut**. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** both systems are limited by their **gamuts** (the range of colors a specific device can produce), and CMY models often restrict the artist to **only three pigments** for the sake of engineering automation — a constraint that may not satisfy a painter's needs. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- MacEvoy's functional paint wheel converges on the CMY insight: magenta, light yellow, and cyan pigments (not schoolbook red and blue) span the widest reachable paint gamut. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]])*

#### 8. The Ostwald Color System (~1916)

**Creator:** Wilhelm Ostwald (c. 1916). **Organizing principle:** order through mixtures of hue, black, and white.

- Ostwald used **rotating Maxwell discs** to determine regular relationships between these components, organizing colors into **triangles of "like hue"** by their degree of **whiteness (tints), blackness (shades), or grayness (tones)** — useful for industrial standardization. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- **Limitations:** Ostwald's wheel is **unserviceable for painters** because his **diametrically opposed colors** (e.g., blue and yellow) **mix to green in pigments rather than a neutral gray** — and it ignores physiological laws such as simultaneous contrast. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*
- The flaw is structural: the system's geometry comes from **additive-averaging spinning-disc mixtures**, so its opposite relationships are additive complements, not colourant-mixing complements. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/018.md|018.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/073.md|073.html]])*

### Why the disagreement reveals color as arbitrary
![[Pasted image 20260814205417.png]]
The eight systems disagree because each is a **specific tool designed to solve a different problem**:

- **Newton / RGB** explore the **physics of light**;
- **RYB / CMY** attempt to **predict pigment mixtures**;
- **Munsell / CIELAB** prioritize **perceptual spacing**;
- **Goethe / Itten** focus on **subjective harmony**.

The contradiction between them proves that a color wheel is a **human choice about which property to organize around** — not a discovery of an objective structure. As Albert Munsell noted, color classification is a **"labyrinth"** in which **any model is merely a clue** used to navigate the endless varieties of sensation. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges)*

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's verdict on the whole genre is the same conclusion reached from measurement: the color wheel is "a helpful but arbitrary construction that distorts as much as it clarifies the facts of color." He is not against wheels — he built one of the most rigorous ever, with ~80 measured pigments plotted by CIECAM hue angle and chroma — but he insists the wheel is a *rule of thumb for mixing*, not a theory of color. The measured wheel's asymmetry is itself informative: greens and yellows reach higher chroma than reds and violets because the distribution of attainable color follows the luminosity function, not a circle. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color14.md|color14.html]])*

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: the same arbitrariness, judged differently.** Farges' survey and MacEvoy's measurements agree on the deep claim — that color classification is arbitrary, and no wheel is a discovery — but they land on opposite attitudes toward the *useful* wheels. Farges grants the RYB/Itten tradition a charitable reading as a useful "discipline" for limited palettes; MacEvoy and Briggs call the same tradition "obsolete" (color13) and "fabulously anachronistic... creation science" (072). Likewise, Farges' "Homebrew" system places pigments on a **fixed, even 0–360° scaffold**, while MacEvoy's wheel rejects exactly that tidying step: measured paint locations "do not correspond exactly to geometrical symmetry," and forcing them onto evenly spaced spokes is "creating symbolism." Both see the wheel as an arbitrary aid — they disagree on how much of the arbitrariness to keep visible. *(Source: "ALL-IN-ONE Color Wheel System for Painters — Color Theory Resources for Artists" — Florent Farges; [[raw_sources/handprint/color13.md|color13.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/072.md|072.html]])*
