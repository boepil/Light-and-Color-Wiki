**Scope:** Colors that no single wavelength of light produces — the purples, magentas, and deep reds that only arise from mixtures of the two ends of the visible spectrum — and why they still occupy a large, irreducibly perceptual slice of every color circle.

### A hue with no wavelength

A **nonspectral** (or **extraspectral**) color is one for which **no single wavelength of light exists**. Most hues — red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet — are evoked by single wavelengths and are therefore *spectral*. Purple, magenta, and the deep reds are different: they are only produced by **mixtures of the two ends of the spectrum**, short-wave (blue-violet) light combined with long-wave (red) light.

- The sources state it directly: the hues from purples through magentas to deep reds "are called nonspectral because no monochromatic light produces them" — they "require combinations of wavelengths." *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/014.md|014.html]])*
- Consequently the spectrum itself has **no purple in it**: end to end the rainbow runs red through violet, and the gap between its two ends — where the wheel will later close — is empty of spectral light. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/121.md|121.html]])*
- Purples are therefore *not missing from the world*; they are missing from **light**. The brain, as the sources put it, "invents" the magenta hue to fill a perceptual gap that the physics of single wavelengths cannot.

### The line of purples: closing the outer edge of color

On the **[[CIE Systems|CIE chromaticity diagram]]**, the spectral colors trace the **horseshoe-shaped spectral locus** — a solid curved line from violet (~380–400 nm), over the top, down through red (~700–780 nm). The nonspectral colors do not lie on that curve; they fall along the **straight line closing the horseshoe's open end**:

- The science textbooks call that chord the **line of purples** — the straight segment connecting the 400 nm (violet) and 780 nm (red) ends of the locus. *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/118.md|118.html]])*
- "The non-spectral purples formed by mixing the 380 nm blue and 780 nm red wavelengths fall on the straight line connecting the ends of the horseshoe."
- Every color the eye can see is the area bounded *by both* the curved spectral locus *and* this dashed closing chord; the line of purples is thus one of the two outer envelopes of human color vision (the other being the locus itself). See [[Light/Spectral Locus & Excitation Purity|Spectral Locus & Excitation Purity]].
- The chord is **straight** for the same reason the locus is curved: additive mixing of the two end colors sweeps out every intermediate, and mixtures of two lights sit on the straight connecting segment in the diagram.

### Why the spectrum must be bent into a circle

The physical spectrum is **linear** — a one-dimensional strip of wavelengths. Perception, however, is **circular**: the two ends of the strip seem to belong next to each other, joined by purple and magenta. Rolling the linear spectrum into a circle was the foundational move of color diagramming:

- **Newton** in his 1704 *Opticks* was the first to "roll his linear spectrum into a colour circle," diagramming the spectral hues into a closed ring. Newton obtained, as the painter-theorists note, a "continuous color circle" by **supplementing the spectral hues with purple, between red and violet**.
- The spectral sequence thus forms a closed loop "completed with the colors of the magenta-purple line" — a construction that persists, unbroken, into the modern CIE diagram and every hue wheel downstream (see [[Colors/Color Wheel System|Color Wheel System]]).
- The "not a wheel" reality check from a studio source: visible light "is not a wheel, but it can be conceived of as one, with pinks being how our brain interprets the mixing of red and violet." The circle is an act of **perceptual completion**, not a feature of light.

### How big: the nonspectral share of the hue circle

The purples/magentas are not a thin seam — they occupy a strikingly large fraction of perceived hue:

- In the **[[Munsell Notation|Munsell]]** system, **Purple is one of the five principal hues**, claiming a full fifth (20%) of the primary circle — equal in formal status to Red, Yellow, Green, and Blue.
- Cross-system comparison shows the red-to-blue region is disproportionately spacious: while the green-to-yellow region spans only about **21–23 Munsell hue steps**, the red-to-blue region (which contains all the nonspectral purples) spans roughly **35–47 steps** of the 100-step circle. Perceptually, practically all of the "action" between red and blue is occupied by colors with no spectral wavelength.
- In handprint analysis the **extraspectral mixtures span about one fourth of the total circumference** of the perceptual wheel. *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*

### The cone-and-opponent mechanism of "inventing" magenta

Why does a physical gap in the spectrum become a perceptual filled region? The modern answer combines **trichromacy** (three cones) with **opponency** (opposed signals):

- Purple arises from **simultaneous stimulation of the S-cones (short-wave) and L-cones (long-wave)** without significant M-cone (middle/green) activation — a response pattern that a single wavelength cannot produce, because short wavelengths and long wavelengths never co-occur in one monochromatic ray. The textbooks identify the **non-spectral S+L dominant response** as the origin of "the range of non-spectral hues from purple to magenta and middle red." *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/014.md|014.html]])*
- Color, per the opponent model, is computed as **differences between receptor responses** — red–green (r−g) and blue–yellow (y−b). Purple is the pairing *both red and blue* — and the phenomenology is strict about which pairings are possible: purples (red+blue), oranges (red+yellow), blue-greens, and yellow-greens all exist as simultaneous compound experiences, but there is **no color that is subjectively red *and* green, nor blue *and* yellow**.
- The consequence is that the opponent apparatus "invents" magenta to fill the computational gap. In the classic demonstration (the butterfly meadow), blue plus red yields no butterfly in the meadow because there is no pure magenta in the rainbow: the brain **orders and supplements** its colors, filling the gap between red and blue with "new, freely invented colors."

### Complements live here — and here they part from the opponent axes

The sequence of a spectral hue and its true (additive) complement passes *through* the nonspectral region for several hues — which is exactly why the classic opponent pairs (red/green, blue/yellow) do **not** coincide with the additive complement pairs:

- Pairs of lights that make white sit on **opposite sides of any straight line through the white point**: the standard pair **480 nm blue + 580 nm yellow → white**, and **red with cyan**, green with magenta (depending on the exact green), and violet with yellowish-green are all complementary pairs in this additive sense.
- Therefore **red's true complement is a blue-green (cyan, ~488 nm)**, and **the complement of middle green is magenta** — not the red of the traditional wheel. The afterimage complements agree with this at the red-cyan and green-magenta axes: exhaust the red cones and white light appears **cyan**; exhaust the green cones and it appears **magenta**.
- Yet careful measurement shows the opponent axes are the *exception*, not the rule: **opposite hues on a hue-angle circle are only true complements near the yellow-blue, red-cyan, and green-magenta axes.** Away from those axes, and through most of the purple region, "the classic red–green and yellow–blue diametric pairs do not hold empirically." *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/043.md|043.html]])*
- This mismatch is the subject of [[Intersections/Perceptual Complements vs. Mixing Complements|Perceptual Complements vs. Mixing Complements]] — in short, three different complement relationships (additive, opponent, mixing) each thread differently through the nonspectral gap.

### Specifying them: the complementary-wavelength "c" notation

Because nonspectral hues have **no dominant wavelength on their own side** of the diagram, colorimetry specifies them by the wavelength **directly opposite on the hue circle**:

- The standard notation extends the line from the white point through the color to the *opposite* side of the locus and appends **"c"** (for complementary). A purple beam at coordinates (0.33, 0.15) is specified as **540c** — "complementary dominant wavelength 540 nm" — with a purity of 70%.
- Handprint states the working rule: extraspectral hues are "usually denoted by the wavelength number of the hue directly opposite on the hue circle: thus an extraspectral magenta is notated by its complementary 'green' wavelength." *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*

### Natural or artificial? The scattering and interference of pink

Contrary to the pop-science slogan that magenta "does not exist in nature," nonspectral hues recur widely in the natural world — not as *spectral gaps* but as the products of other color mechanisms:

- **Scattering** supplies the blue component in many pinks and purples: pink human skin, the blue in certain greens and purples of butterflies, birds, fishes, and monkeys, and the red-purples of **sunset clouds**.
- **Interference** (structural color) produces iridescent purples and magentas in hummingbird feathers, soap films, and iridescent clouds.
- **Minerals** deliver them directly: amethyst and pink morganite are everyday natural nonspectral hues.
- The constraint is narrower and precise: no *single wavelength* is purple, so no rainbow (a pure dispersion of wavelengths) shows it — but any reflecting or scattering surface combining the right red and blue content produces it.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy treats the extraspectral hues as a large, practical quarter of the wheel used every time a painter reaches for a warm red or mixed violet. His key observations: (1) notations — extraspectral hues "are awkward to specify" by wavelength and are "usually denoted" by the complement opposite on the hue circle, exactly as the "c" notation does; (2) scale — "the extraspectral mixtures span one fourth of the total circumference" even in Newton's own diagram, a proportion modern perceptual scaling retains; (3) chromatic status — while spectral hues are "the most saturated color stimuli possible," a "pure" red is actually **not visible in the spectrum at all** — red, like magenta, is itself an extraspectral convention, mixed from the spectral ends; and (4) warmth — in his warmth/coolness system, hue band 8 (red-violet / magenta) stands inside the warm-cool hybrid zone where "blue and red visibly mix," which is why magenta reads neither clearly warm nor clearly cool. Notably, he frames the perceptual circle as the *necessary* device: only by bending the spectrum through the extraspectral gap can hue acquire the circularity the other attributes lack *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: "magenta doesn't exist in nature" vs. magenta everywhere in nature.** The popular framing — "no pure magenta or purple in nature! ... magenta is merely an invention of our tricolor vision system" — is true only in the narrow sense that *no single wavelength* is magenta. Strictly, **every** hue is an achievement of the brain (all colors are "made up" the same way, as opponent combinations of receptor responses), so magenta is not uniquely artificial. And in the practical sense the claim is simply false: scattering, interference, and mineral chemistry produce pinks and purples all around us — from sunset clouds and human skin to hummingbird feathers and amethyst. The corrected statement is: **purple exists in nature; it just never exists in a single wavelength of light.** *(Sources disagree: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/037.md|037.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/115.md|115.html]] vs. the sunset-scattering / amethyst claims of [[raw_sources/handprint/color18a.md|color18a.html]])*

## Subtopics
- Nonspectral definition: no single wavelength; purples, magentas, deep reds from mixing the spectral ends
- Line of purples as the straight closing chord of the CIE spectral locus (400 nm → 780 nm)
- Newton 1704: rolling the linear spectrum into a circle by inserting purple between red and violet
- Scale: Purple = 1/5 of Munsell's principal hues; red-to-blue spans ~35–47 vs green-to-yellow ~21–23 steps; ~1/4 of the circumference extraspectral
- Mechanism: S+L cone response without M; opponent r−g / y−b differences; no subjectively red-*and*-green or blue-*and*-yellow
- Complements: additive (480 nm + 580 nm → white; red ↔ cyan ~488 nm; green ↔ magenta) vs. opponent axes, and their disagreement through the purple region
- The "c" notation (e.g. 540c) for specifying nonspectral dominant channels
- Natural nonspectral colors: scattering (pink skin, sunset purples), interference (iridescence), minerals (amethyst, morganite)
- Nonspectral pigments: quinacridone magenta (PR122), dioxazine violet (PV23), alizarin crimson, cobalt violet
