**Scope:** The physical optics of pigment powders — particle size, refractive index, tinting strength, and crystal polymorphism.

### Particle Size, Color, and Opacity

- **Size shifts hue:** lighter cadmium sulfide shades have smaller particle distributions; coarser particles run "deeper oranges"; overgrinding moves a chrome orange masstone toward yellow. Coarse particles under the microscope often appear "more brilliant and colorful," but fine grinding is needed "to ensure each particle is uniformly coated by the binder" (*Artists' Pigments*, *The Science of Paintings*).
- **Refractive index difference drives opacity:** titanium white's high index (2.5–2.7) vs. linseed oil (1.5) scatters light — opaque film; ultramarine's index (~1.5, ≈ oil) lets light pass — transparent in oil. In watercolor/gouache pigments gain opacity because the dry medium (or air) has a much lower index, enlarging the optical difference (*The Science of Paintings*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Granulation:** large/heavy particles "settle into the 'grain' or tiny valleys of the paper," giving washes a characteristic "sparkle" and texture (*Mastering Atmosphere & Mood*).

### Tinting Strength and Its Measurement

**Tinting strength** is "a pigment's inherent ability to influence the hue of another color — usually a white base — when mixed together" (*Ralph Mayer*). Measurement methods:

- **Rub-out test:** mull a weighed pigment (e.g., 0.1 g) with a standard amount of zinc oxide (e.g., 2 g); for extreme organics like phthalocyanine blue a reduction ratio up to **50:1** is used to disclose tone qualities (*Ralph Mayer*).
- **Volumetric method:** since painters work by volume, "10 or 20 parts white to 1 part color" is more practical in studio practice (*Plein Air Techniques*).
- **Instrumental standards:** the National Bureau of Standards uses **daylight reflectance (Y values)** to quantify tinting strength and keep batches consistent with the prototype (*The Science of Paintings*).

### Crystal Polymorphism

**Polymorphism:** one chemical substance "existing in more than one crystal system or form, with each modification potentially possessing different colors and physical properties" (*The Science of Paintings*).

- **Phthalocyanine blue (PB15):** **alpha form** (PB15, 15:1, 15:2) — "clean, bright red-shade blue," the least stable "meta" form, shifting greener under strong solvents or heat; **beta form** (PB15:3, 15:4) — "clean green-shade or peacock blue," the most stable, resistant to recrystallization (*Artists' Pigments*).
- **Cadmium sulfide:** hexagonal (alpha) vs. cubic (beta) structures (*Artists' Pigments*).
- **Chrome yellow (lead chromate):** monoclinic (medium yellows) vs. orthorhombic (lighter primrose yellows) (*The Science of Paintings*).
- **Vermilion (mercuric sulfide):** red in the stable **cinnabar** form but transforms to black **metacinnabar** under light (*Artists' Pigments*, *Color for Science, Art and Technology*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's central doctrine is that the physical pigment — "particle size and specific gravity" — matters "just as much as its hue": cobalt blue and ultramarine's heavy particles settle into paper valleys for granulation, while "finely milled synthetic organics stain the paper fibers evenly." He flags the abstract-hue blind spot: traditional color theory "entirely ignores the physical realities of the paint" — combining a granulating pigment with a staining one produces "distinct visual separations and textural behaviors that simple, abstract color wheels cannot predict." Polychrome behavior (the PB15 α→β shift, vermilion→metacinnabar) is precisely why he insists painters buy chemically identified pigments and test them in their own systems *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt3.md|pigmt3.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt1.md|pigmt1.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: Abstract Hue vs. Physical Pigment Attributes**
