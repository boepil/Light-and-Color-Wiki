**Scope:** How paints are classified as transparent, semi-transparent, semi-opaque, or opaque; the label vocabulary (masstone, undertone, tinting strength); and the complete decoding of everything printed on a tube of artist paint — Color Index names, constitution numbers, series numbers, and lightfastness ratings.

### The Physics of Transparency and Opacity

Whether a paint film hides or transmits depends on the pigment particles suspended in the binder:

- **Refractive index difference (Δn):** opacity is primarily the difference in refractive index between pigment and binder. **[[PB29 - Ultramarine Blue|Ultramarine]]** (n ≈ 1.50) in linseed oil (n ≈ 1.48) is transparent — indices nearly match, light passes with minimal scattering; **[[PW6 - Titanium White|titanium white]]** (n ≈ 2.71) in the same oil is intensely opaque. The vehicle matters too: water (1.33) vs. oil (1.48) changes the Δn and with it the perceived transparency.
- **Particle size:** scattering is most effective when particles are roughly the size of visible-light wavelengths (~500 nm). Large crystals can be transparent; ground to the ~500 nm range they become highly opaque.
- **Painter's use of the classes:**
  - **Transparent** paints — glazed in thin washes over dry color to "sway" or vivify the color beneath.
  - **Semi-transparent / semi-opaque** — the largest workhorse class: they both tint and veil.
  - **Opaque** paints — for scumbling (a thin layer over dry paint to gray a color cast), for covering dark passages, and for the final light accents; multiple passes are needed when painting opaque color over dark grounds.

### Masstone, Undertone, Tinting Strength

- **Masstone (mass color):** the color of a thick, undiluted layer that completely hides the surface — **[[PR83 - Alizarin Crimson|alizarin crimson]]**'s deep maroon masstone vs. its ruby-red undertone.
- **Undertone:** the color revealed when the pigment is spread thinly or viewed in transmitted light; it is often the more saturated color, and the one that governs tinted mixtures.
- **Tinting strength:** the power of a pigment to influence another color (usually white) in a mixture. Tested by "rub-outs": a weighed pigment (e.g., 0.1 g) mulled with white (e.g., 2.0 g zinc oxide); for extremely strong modern organics like **[[PB15 - Phthalo Blue|phthalocyanine blue]]** the reduction ratio is extended to **50:1** to disclose tonal character.

### Reading the Tube: The Anatomy of a Paint Label

Standardized labeling follows the **CS98-62 Paint Standard** and **ASTM D4236** (health labeling) ([[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]]):

- **Pigment common name** — "Cadmium Red", "Ultramarine Blue".
- **Color Index generic name** — the international identifier, e.g. PR108 = Pigment Red 108, PB29 = Pigment Blue 29.
- **Color Index constitution number** — a five-digit number (e.g., 77196 for PR108, 77007 for PB29) designating chemical structure; withheld when a manufacturer's recipe is proprietary. Numbers 77000–77999 cover inorganics.
- **Series number** — a manufacturer's pricing band: low series for cheap earths, high series for costly cadmiums and cobalts.
- **Lightfastness rating** — the ASTM category (I "Excellent", II "Very Good", III, IV) or the manufacturer's stars; the industrial **Blue Wool scale** runs 1 (fugitive) to 8 (extremely lightfast) with exposure-time equivalents per level ([[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]]).
- **Health warning / ACMI seal** — certification that the product meets the Art and Creative Materials Institute safety standard (ASTM D4236).

### The Color Index Naming Structure

- A letter prefix gives the general category (P = pigment), then a hue-family letter: **R** red, **Y** yellow, **B** blue, **G** green, **V** violet, **O** orange, **W** white, **Br** brown, **Bk** black — followed by a number: PR101, PY35, PB29, PG7, PV23...
- **Generic name vs. constitution number:** the generic name (PB29) identifies the pigment's application type; the constitution number (77007) identifies its chemistry. Two chemically different pigments never share a generic name; the same chemistry may have several generic names when it serves different applications ([[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]]).
- Suffixes refine variants: **PB15:3** (beta, green-shade phthalo) vs. PB15:6 (red-shade); PY35 vs. PY35:1 (cadmium zinc sulfide lemon).

### Which Lightfastness Rating to Trust

- The **ASTM** ratings are the most rigorous benchmarks, derived from months of exposure to direct southern sunlight; the **CS98-62 Paint Standard** and ASTM D01.57 define the categories.
- **Manufacturer ratings** may be based on the pigment maker's data, chemical reasoning, or tests of a different paint form — not always on the tube's actual paint ([[raw_sources/handprint/litetest.md|litetest.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]]).
- **Handprint's own verdict:** published lightfastness ratings — including ASTM's — are systematically unreliable: the same generic pigment can receive very different ratings across brands; dilution changes fade rate; tests average brand differences away. MacEvoy rates paints from his own 1999/2004 sunlight exposures and urges artists to treat every published rating as advisory only *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/litetest.md|litetest.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt8.md|pigmt8.html]])*.

### Opacity, Granulation, and Staining Symbols

- **Opacity symbols** printed on tubes/brochures: filled square = opaque, half-filled = semi-opaque, empty square = transparent (some lines use filled/empty circles the same way).
- **Granulation** marks a pigment whose coarse particles settle into paper valleys, producing texture (**[[PG18 - Viridian|viridian]]**, ultramarine); **staining** marks pigments that penetrate paper fibers and cannot be lifted (phthalos, quinacridones) — both appear as small icons or letters on modern labels ([[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt3.md|pigmt3.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]]).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy's paint guides are built on exactly the information this page decodes: every pigment evaluation he publishes begins with a one-line behavior summary — "**[[PR254 - Pyrrole Red|pyrrole red PR254]]** is a very lightfast, semiopaque, highly staining, dark valued, intense medium red pigment" — combining opacity, staining, value, and strength in a single sentence. He is scathing about the label's trustworthiness, however: many reputable brands omit pigment info or lightfastness ratings entirely, and he requires "conforms to ASTM-D5067" labeling assurance before he trusts any rating. His opacity tests use an indelible black line under the paint film — the difference between painted and unpainted line is the true measure of hiding *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt8.md|pigmt8.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt7.md|pigmt7.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/intstud.md|intstud.html]])*.

## HueValueChroma Perspectives

Briggs lays the physical mechanism under the transparency classes and turns opacity into a *mixing* quantity:

- **Why transparent paints always darken a mixture.** "Opaque colours may lighten or darken a mixture, but transparent colours always darken": a transparent colourant works purely by subtractively removing light and never scatters it back, while an opaque paint's *conditional* effect balances wavelength absorption against its role as an added reflector. This is the same physics as the page's Δn rule, recast as a rule of mixture outcome *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/063.md|063.html]])*.
- **A transparent paint has no single Munsell notation.** Used as a glaze, a transparent colour "glazed over white can have any value from its body colour (very dark for most of them) up to near white" — it is not a point but a path in colour space. Jacketing stops claiming one masstone/undertone pair captures it: the perceived hue *also* drifts with layer thickness toward the pigment's **undertone** (a magenta reads bluish thin / reddish thick) *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/063.md|063.html]])*.
- **Glazed purity beats white-dilution.** Transparent colour glazed over white reaches a **higher maximum chroma** than the same colour physically mixed with white paint, because glazing keeps light travelling through pure colourant rather than interspersing scattering white particles. This is the measured justification for the glazing-first workflow this page describes *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/063.md|063.html]])*.
- **Opaque = high-chroma near-neighbours only.** Opacity also shapes the pigment's *mixing gamut*: paints far from the ideal subtractive (CMY) primaries — e.g. opaque **[[PR108 - Cadmium Red|cadmium red]]** and **[[PB28 - Cobalt Blue|cobalt blue]]** — "make high-chroma mixtures with only a few adjacent hues," whereas paints close to the primaries mix high-chroma across distant hues. An opaque class is therefore not just a hiding-power label but a predictor of how the colour behaves in chromatic mixtures *(Source: [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/061.md|061.html]], [[raw_sources/huevaluechroma/063.md|063.html]])*.

> [!NOTE] The page's masstone/undertone pair describes a *snapshot* of the paint; Briggs' glaze-path account explains how a single transparent pigment can legitimately populate the whole span between them — the two vocabularies describe the same phenomenon at different scales.
