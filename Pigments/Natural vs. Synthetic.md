**Scope:** Natural vs. synthetic pigments — the geological/biological origins of natural colorants, the 19th-century manufactured revolution, and today's trade-offs of subtlety against chroma.

### Natural Pigment Sources

- **Minerals and earths:** "native earths" — clays colored by **iron and manganese oxides**; ochre, sienna, umber used since prehistory ("cave paintings dating back to 10,500 B.C."); the finest ochres traditionally mined in France, siennas and umbers from Italian deposits (*Artists' Pigments*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Plants:** **madder** — a vegetable red from the root of *Rubia tinctorium*, used since ancient Egypt and Greece; **indigo** — a deep blue from Indian plants, so vital that Roman writers "sometimes mistook its compressed blocks for minerals" (*Artists' Pigments*, *Contemporary Color*).
- **Animals:** **carmine and kermes** — red lakes from scale insects (kermes "one of the oldest known organic colorants"; cochineal brought to Europe from the New World by the Spanish in the 16th century); **Indian yellow** — from the urine of cows fed exclusively on mango leaves, "prohibited on humane grounds in 1908" (*Artists' Pigments*, *Color in Art*).

### The 19th-Century Synthetic Revolution

- **Cost:** **synthetic ultramarine (1828, Guimet and Gmelin)** replaced "the 'most precious' and prohibitively expensive natural ultramarine made from ground lapis lazuli" (*Artists' Pigments*, *A Comprehensive Overview...*).
- **Hue gamut:** chromium (1797) and cadmium (1817) discoveries produced **chrome yellows and cadmium yellows** with "a level of brightness previously unattainable"; Perkin's **mauve (1856)** launched the coal-tar dye industry, followed by **alizarin crimson (1868)** replacing natural madder (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Contemporary Color*).
- **Permanence problems:** early synthetics lacked lightfastness — chrome yellows "tended to darken or turn green with age," and many early "aniline" colors were "notoriously fugitive," disparaged by traditionalists (*The Science of Paintings*, *Color in Art*).

### Modern Trade-offs: Subtlety vs. Chroma

- **Natural earths** survive for "their delicate and subtle tones, which are often lost in more powerful artificial substitutes," plus a "granular texture or grittiness — seen in green earth or historical Naples yellow — that provides a characteristic 'signature' to the paint film" (*Artists' Pigments*, *The Science of Paintings*).
- **Synthetic organics** — quinacridones, phthalocyanines — deliver "extreme intensity and high chroma" through "spectral purity, reflecting only a very narrow band of light," and — unlike early synthetics — "remarkable resistance to fading, often equalling or exceeding the permanence of traditional inorganic pigments" (*A Comprehensive Overview..., Ralph Mayer*).

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy reads the natural-to-synthetic arc as the motor of modern palette design: the late-19th-century flood of manufactured pigments was driven by consumer demand for brilliant color — "artists wanted brilliant pigments in order to appease the rising demand for dramatically colored paintings" — while lightfastness was often "not scrutinized," and "many artists denied or downplayed the permanency issue" (Turner's famous retort to William Winsor; the Russell–Abney 1888 report that settled the debate). His verdict is pragmatic, not nostalgic: he treats "natural" pigment origin as irrelevant to mixing behavior — what matters is measured hue, value, chroma, and handling attributes (granulation vs. staining) — which is why his palettes mix classical earths with engineered synthetics on evidence, not sentiment *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt6.md|pigmt6.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt1.md|pigmt1.html]])*.

> [!WARNING] **Contradiction Flag: "Natural" as Quality Label**
> The corpus uses "natural" partly as provenance (mineral/plant/animal origin) and partly as a market label implying permanence or subtlety — while several natural lakes (carmine, Indian yellow) are among the most fugitive colorants ever used, and several synthetics (quinacridones, phthalocyanines) out-perform them. The label alone settles neither chroma nor permanence.
