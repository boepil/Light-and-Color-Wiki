**Scope:** Prussian Blue (PB27) — ferric ferrocyanide, the first fully synthetic pigment with a documented history, the affordable deep blue of Turner, Whistler, Picasso, and Hokusai's Great Wave.

### Identity

- **Colour Index:** PB27; **ferric ferrocyanide, (Fe³⁺)₄[Feᴵᴵ(CN)₆]₃** — some modern industrial varieties ("iron blue") are ferric ammonium ferrocyanide.
- Color arises from a **charge-transfer mechanism** between Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ ions in the crystal lattice.
- A deep, intense **cyan or greenish blue**; at full strength (masstone) it is "so dark it approximates black," revealing a distinctly **greenish undertone** when reduced with white.

### History

- Discovered **accidentally by Diesbach in Berlin around 1704–1706** — a color maker who stumbled on the blue substance while attempting a red pigment with contaminated materials.
- The **first synthetic pigment with a completely documented history**; its process was kept secret until published by **Woodward in England in 1724**.
- Entered the artist's palette roughly **20 years after discovery** and spread rapidly across Europe and Asia.

### Production

Manufactured by **chemical precipitation**. Historically "let down" with inert bases: **Antwerp blue** = one part Prussian blue mixed with three parts alumina hydrate. High-quality pure grades sell as **Chinese blue or Milori blue**.

### Color Data

- **Naturally transparent**, but because of its extreme tinting power and depth it functions as an opaque pigment in heavy layers.
- **Concentrated films often show a coppery or metallic bronze sheen** on the surface.
- **Extremely high tinctorial power** — frequently reduced by manufacturers with **50–75% alumina hydrate or blanc fixe** to make it manageable.
- **Permanence: "borderline."** Destroyed by alkalis (unusable in fresco or with alkaline materials); generally stable in oil but may turn brownish with age. Its signature quirk: it may **fade in thin glazes or tints under light yet recover its original intensity when stored in the dark**; in oxidizable vehicles like linseed oil it can bleach.
- **Drying:** a **rapid drier in oil** producing a hard film; difficult to grind buttery, tending to agglomerate or become granular.

### Relation to Art and Painters

- Revolutionary as a **deep, transparent blue far cheaper than natural ultramarine**; augmented and eventually replaced azurite and unsatisfactory copper-based blues. On the modern palette it has been largely superseded by **[[PB15 - Phthalo Blue|phthalocyanine blue]]**, which shares its intensity with superior lightfastness.
- **J.M.W. Turner** used it extensively, notably in his "cool" paintings of the 1830s such as *The Burning of the Houses of Lords and Commons*.
- **Monet and the Impressionists** used it for atmospheric effects and "colored shadows" — blue rather than gray-black.
- **Whistler** used it in his tonal studies and nocturnes (*Nocturne: Blue and Gold*).
- **Picasso** used it as a staple of his **Blue Period** (c. 1901–1904) to convey sorrow and emptiness.
- **Katsushika Hokusai** used it in **"The Great Wave off Kanagawa"** (c. 1830–1832) — its intense, unmodulated flat blue areas helped shape European Post-Impressionism (Van Gogh, Gauguin).
- **Goethe** placed it on the "minus side" of his color scale, noting its intense blackness when condensed and its metallic surface shine.

## Handprint Perspectives

MacEvoy treats Prussian blue as a historically crucial but "quirky" modern pigment: its extreme tinting strength makes it a classic "sparing" palette color, its greenish undertone gives it the strong mixing value of a cyan, and its copper-bronze surface sheen is a physical tell. He pairs its fading-in-light / recovering-in-dark behavior with the broader warning that published permanence ratings are unreliable, and notes the pigment survives in modern palettes mainly as a niche deep blue now that phthalo blue (PB15) supplies the same intensity with better lightfastness *(Source: [[raw_sources/handprint/pigmt1.md|pigmt1.html]], [[raw_sources/handprint/color16.md|color16.html]])*.
