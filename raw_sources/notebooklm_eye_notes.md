Human color vision is a complex biological process that translates physical electromagnetic radiation into psychological experiences through distinct anatomical and neural stages.

### 1. Eye Anatomy
The visual process begins when light enters the **cornea**, a transparent bulge at the front of the eye that provides primary focusing power and protection (**Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). Light then passes through the **aqueous humor** and the **pupil**, an opening in the circular **iris** that functions like a camera's aperture by constricting or dilating to regulate light levels (**Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). Behind the iris is the **lens**, which adjusts its shape—a process called **accommodation**—via the ciliary muscles to focus objects at various distances (**Color Management**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). 

After traveling through the transparent **vitreous humor**, the light strikes the **retina**, the neural membrane lining the back of the eyeball (**Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). The retina contains approximately 126 million photoreceptors that convert photons into electrical nerve impulses (**Illusions of Seeing**). The **fovea centralis** is a tiny pit at the center of the retina containing a high density of cones but no rods, making it the region of highest color acuity and sharpest vision (**Illusions of Seeing**, **Color Management**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). 

### 2. Wavelength Perception by Cones
Color vision is founded on the **Trichromatic Theory**, which posits that the retina uses three distinct types of cone photoreceptors sensitive to different portions of the spectrum (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). While roughly 120 million **rods** operate in low light for monochromatic night vision, approximately 6 to 7 million **cones** mediate color perception in daylight (**Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

*   **Short-wavelength (S) cones** are sensitive to blue-violet light, with peak sensitivity at approximately 440–448 nm (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). They are the rarest type, making up only 5–10% of the cone population (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
*   **Medium-wavelength (M) cones** peak in the green region at approximately 518–540 nm (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
*   **Long-wavelength (L) cones** are sensitive to red-orange light, peaking at approximately 560–617 nm (**Contemporary Color**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

Each cone follows the **principle of univariance**, meaning a single receptor's output is a scalar value that cannot distinguish between changes in wavelength and changes in light intensity (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). The brain determines color by comparing the relative patterns of activation across these three overlapping cone systems (**The Science of Paintings**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

### 3. Opponent-Process Theory
The **Opponent-Process Theory** describes the second stage of color vision, where signals from the cones are neurally encoded into antagonistic pairs: **red-green**, **blue-yellow**, and **black-white** (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). This antagonistic processing begins in the retina's bipolar and ganglion cells and continues to the **lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN)** of the brain (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **Contemporary Color**, **Illusions of Seeing**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

*   **Antagonistic Pairs:** The red-green channel is computed as the difference between L and M cone excitations (L–M), while the blue-yellow channel compares S-cone input against the combined sum of L and M cones (S–(L+M)) (**A Comprehensive Overview**, **Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). 
*   **Phenomenological Effects:** This system ensures we cannot perceive a color as being simultaneously "reddish-green" (**vision-science-photons-to-pheno**). It also accounts for **complementary afterimages**; staring at a saturated color "tires out" or habituates one side of an opponent pair, causing the balance to shift toward the opponent hue when looking at a neutral surface (**Color Vision: Trichromatic and Opponent Process Theories**, **Contemporary Color**, **The Art of Color**, **The Science of Paintings**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).

Modern **Dual Process (or Zone) theories** reconcile these views by demonstrating they describe sequential stages: trichromatic processing occurs at the receptor level, while opponent processing occurs at the neural level (**Illustrating Color Evolution and Color Blindness**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **vision-science-photons-to-pheno**).
