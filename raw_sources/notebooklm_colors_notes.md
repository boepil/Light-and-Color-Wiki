Color coordinate systems and gamuts provide the mathematical and empirical frameworks necessary to standardize, measure, and reproduce color across different media and industries.

### 1. CIE Systems: XYZ, xy, and Lab
The **Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE)** established the first international standards for colorimetry in 1931 to replace subjective identification with mathematical algorithms (**Contemporary Color**).

*   **CIE XYZ and Standard Observers:** The system was founded on color-matching experiments where observers matched spectral wavelengths using three primaries. Because real RGB primaries required "negative" light to match certain saturated colors, the CIE created imaginary "ideal" primaries—**X, Y, and Z**—to ensure all color-matching functions remained positive (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). The **CIE 1931 2° Standard Observer** represents the foveal region of the eye, while the **1964 10° Standard Observer** was developed for larger viewing fields typical in industrial settings (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Color Management**).
*   **CIE xy Chromaticity Diagram:** This 2D diagram is a projection of the 3D XYZ space where $x$ and $y$ coordinates are calculated from tristimulus values (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). It features a horseshoe-shaped **spectral locus** representing pure monochromatic lights from 380 nm to 780 nm (**The Science of Paintings**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). The central **"E" or "W" point** represents the achromatic white point (**Contemporary Color**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**).
*   **CIELAB (L*a*b*):** Introduced in 1976, this space was designed to be more **perceptually uniform** than xy space (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). It utilizes an **opponent-color** framework: **L*** represents lightness (0 to 100), **a*** represents the red-green axis, and **b*** represents the yellow-blue axis (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Contemporary Color**). It is a **device-independent** model, acting as a "universal translator" between different hardware platforms (**Contemporary Color**).

### 2. Munsell Notation and the 1943 Renotation
Devised by Albert H. Munsell in 1905, this system organizes color into three independent perceptual dimensions: **Hue, Value, and Chroma** (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **gutenberg.org-A COLOR NOTATION.pdf**).

*   **Hue (H):** The spectral quality, organized into five principal hues (Red, Yellow, Green, Blue, Purple) and five intermediate hues (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **Contemporary Color**).
*   **Value (V):** Lightness, running from 0/ (ideal black) to 10/ (ideal white) (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **Contemporary Color**).
*   **Chroma (C):** The degree of departure from a neutral gray of the same value; unlike saturation, it is an open-ended scale to accommodate new, high-chroma pigments (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **gutenberg.org-A COLOR NOTATION.pdf**).
*   **1943 Munsell Renotation:** To correct inconsistencies in the original system, a large-scale visual experiment involving 3 million observations was conducted to provide precise **CIE (Y, x, y) coordinates** for each Munsell chip (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **Munsell Color Science Lab...**). This Renotation showed that constant-chroma loci expand dramatically at very dark values, meaning dark colors can theoretically achieve very high saturation (**A Comprehensive Overview...**).

### 3. MacAdam Ellipses and Limits
*   **MacAdam Ellipses:** These represent regions on the CIE chromaticity diagram where colors are visually indistinguishable from a central color; any color falling within the ellipse is perceived as an identical match to the human eye (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**, **Contemporary Color**). Their varying sizes across the diagram highlight the **lack of visual uniformity** in the original CIE xy space (**color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**).
*   **Optimal Color Solid (MacAdam Limits):** These are the theoretical physical boundaries for non-fluorescent surface colors (**Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red...**). These limits describe "optimal colors" with step-function reflectance spectra (0% or 100%) (**A Comprehensive Overview...**, **Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red...**). The resulting solid is **asymmetric**: because the human luminosity function peaks in the green-yellow region (555 nm), green hues can achieve much higher chroma at high lightness levels than red hues can (**Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red...**).

### 4. Gamuts: Pointer's and Device-Specific
A **gamut** is the actual range of color that a specific system or material can reproduce (**Contemporary Color**).

*   **Pointer's Gamut:** Established in a landmark 1980 study, this gamut represents the maximum achievable range of **real surface colors** (paints, inks, textiles, etc.) under Illuminant C (**Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red...**, **Munsell Color Science Lab...**). It confirms that the real-world color space is smaller than the MacAdam limits and remains systematically smaller in the red region compared to the green region (**Why Material Reality Favors Green Over Red...**).
*   **Device Gamuts:** 
    *   **Additive (RGB):** Used by monitors and displays; the gamut is typically a triangle within the CIE diagram defined by the chromaticities of the red, green, and blue phosphors or filters (**Contemporary Color**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). 
    *   **Subtractive (CMYK):** Used in printing; these gamuts are smaller and irregularly shaped due to the absorption limitations of physical pigments and substrates (**Contemporary Color**, **color-for-science-art-and-technology.pdf**). 
    *   **Management:** To bridge these disparate gamuts, **ICC profiles** are used to translate color data from a device's native space into a device-independent space like CIELAB, then back to a different device's gamut (**Contemporary Color**).
